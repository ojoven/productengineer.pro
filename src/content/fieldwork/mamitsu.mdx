## Who and what

[Unai Esteibar](https://uesteibar.com/) is a developer, currently living in Barcelona, who always has a side project going. Most end up in the bin. [Mamitsu](https://usemamitsu.com) did not.

**Mamitsu is an API and MCP server that watches social media videos and returns what you ask for as JSON.** You send a link to an Instagram, TikTok, YouTube or X post and a question in plain words. It watches the video, listens to the audio, reads the caption and answers in the same request. A cooking reel becomes a recipe with ingredients and steps; a travel video becomes a list of places. Apps call it through a REST API. Agents such as Claude Code or Codex call it through MCP, the protocol agents use to connect to tools. Unai builds it on his own, with agents, in his spare time.

I saw Mamitsu on Twitter, loved it and sent Unai some feedback. On 13 November we talked for almost two hours. I had questions ready by territory of the Skills Map. We did not follow them in order, so this write-up groups what came up by territory, not by minute, and ends with what we talked about beyond Mamitsu.

## Discovery: from a coffee grinder to video data

Mamitsu is a side project that came out of another side project: a coffee app. Unai brews each new coffee bag from a base recipe and adjusts it day by day: a bit finer, a bit more water. He built a recipe player that guides you step by step and tells you what to change tomorrow. Then it got out of hand: he normalised grinders, so a recipe for one grinder translates to yours. One of his goes by numbers, another by clicks. "It looks easy. It is a mess." The app could also import recipes from YouTube and Instagram videos, held together with tape.

That import is what the beta testers wanted. One friend wanted it for family recipes, another for his own coffee app, a third for a travel app. What they had in common was not coffee. **It was useful information locked inside videos.**

I asked who else does this. Unai named Supadata and PostReef, with Apify as the generic version; Mamitsu also has comparison pages against TwelveLabs and ScrapeCreators.

## Strategy: restraint and a cheaper bet

Unai gave himself two weeks to package it. He wanted to learn how to turn something like this into a product he would feel confident selling: one that works, holds load and is easy to use from an app or an agent. The products he admires, he says, share one thing: restraint.

The cut that says most is transcripts. **His bet is that the rawer the content, the easier it is to pull out whatever you need.** The quantity in a recipe may only appear on screen; the name of a place may only be said out loud. So the model gets the video, not a transcript. And that has become very cheap.

The other bet is price. Supadata has traction, so: "People use this. Can I do it as well or better, and much cheaper?" He sent me the [comparison page](https://usemamitsu.com/compare/mamitsu-vs-supadata) while we talked.

I suggested another: trend analysis. Finding what is trending on TikTok and Instagram is one problem; working out why it works is the one Mamitsu can solve.

## Engineering: from blocked to 3,000 requests a minute

Selling with confidence meant holding load. The platforms he downloads from used to block him after about five requests in half a minute. Now Mamitsu takes 3,000 TikTok requests a minute and, in his words, they do not care. The first version only read YouTube and Instagram; TikTok came with the product, and the day before our call an email to users announced X support and a playground inside the app.

## AI: a model that watches, agents that build

The product is a model call with a lot of care around it: frames, audio and caption in, JSON in the shape you asked for out, in the same request. Through the MCP server, an agent gets back only the fields it asked for, "so a ten-minute video costs a few lines of context instead of a full transcript", as the site puts it. The [agent plugins](https://github.com/usemamitsu/agent-plugins) are on GitHub.

How does he know the answers are good? He does not measure it yet. Evals cost money, and at this volume he can read extractions himself. When he does start, comparing with a larger model shows where two models disagree, not which one is right. I would also check a sample against the video itself.

The team behind the product is agents. Unai runs Hermes on a Linux box at home, with nine or ten profiles split by project so one context does not leak into another. He talks to all of them in Slack. He dislikes sessions that start from zero; with an agent that remembers, a Slack thread works like talking to one more colleague. For code, Hermes launches Pi agents with a leaner setup, because he wants code written more predictably. He does not review every change. Which ones he reviews is decided per change, by risk.

## Shipping: a risk score decides who approves

Moving tickets in Linear feels unnatural to Unai, so the pipeline lives in Slack. It starts with a conversation with Hermes about the feature and, roughly, the technical solution. He cares most about database migrations and how to roll back: that keeps the blast radius small. Then the agents take over. Hermes hands the work to Pi agents, gets back code and proof that it works, and opens the PR.

Otherwise he gets a Slack message saying all is well. The risk scoring is his own skill. **Two years ago, a product with these processes would have needed a team.**

## Distribution: getting cited by AI answers

The launch was quiet. Traction? "A tiny bit." New users trickle in and the product improved a lot in the week after launch. Next is a wider relaunch, Hacker News included.

The channel he works on most is GEO, generative engine optimisation: being cited by AI answers. "Build it and they will come" was never true, he says, but the coming part has changed. The day before our call he needed somewhere to centralise his agents' MCP servers. He asked his agent to find and compare alternatives, and decided there, without opening a single landing page until after. "I had a decision right there, because I had everything I needed to make it."

When you ask ChatGPT something, it runs your search plus many variations, opens what looks relevant and answers with citations. **A citation means "this is where I read it", not "I recommend this".** And unlike Google, nobody shows you what people ask. So you simulate it.

There are two ways into an answer. One is to be on the pages that already get cited. For another site of his, a directory that AI answers cited contradicted his own site. Search engines do not care; AI answers do, because the model cannot tell which version to believe. The other way is to get cited yourself, with guides, comparison pages and docs. An agent drafts them from the gaps, and he reads everything before it goes out: "I don't like publishing things that have not been through me."

He tracks all this in Omnia, a tool that monitors AI answers, and showed me Mamitsu's numbers there. The method does not depend on the tool.

## Measurement: what he does not track yet

The website has web analytics: Rybbit, self-hosted. Inside the product he tracks nothing yet. Does he look at what people ask Mamitsu? Now and then, by hand. Feedback comes through the support email.

At the end I asked what he would take from the call. An agent that reads the week's extractions and reports every Monday, cheap at the current traffic. It could run the first evals too: compare answers with a larger model, and spot uses he had not planned for. I added a question for that report: which extractions bring users back.

## Beyond the product

Half the call was about product engineering in general. What stayed with me:

### Code is solved, if you pay. Product is not.

"If you are willing to pay for it, it is solved." Only the economics are still settling. Product is another story. The skill that matters more now, for Unai, was always there: knowing what is important, simplifying, choosing how to attack a problem. And one field has no answers yet: experiences augmented with AI. The labs change the format of their products every few days. It will settle, the way all dashboards ended up looking alike, but not yet.

### Demos instead of explanations

Unai used to explain his ideas, badly. Now he often builds a two-hour demo and sends that. Many ideas are built in a morning and binned by the afternoon.

### Take the product to where people already are

It will be harder and harder to bring people to new places, he thinks, so products will go where people already are: WhatsApp, Telegram, the assistant they already talk to. His example is AliExpress parcel tracking on WhatsApp. Nobody wants the app; a message about your parcel works. I live this with Etengabe, my Basque-learning app: with a free minute, I open Twitter, not a learning app.

### Software for agents, and the risk of being sherlocked

I am starting to think about building for agents rather than for people; I no longer open my calendar, I send a voice message. Mamitsu is in that market, a service other agents call. The risk Unai sees: a product built on the labs' models, or for their agents, gets copied when a lab ships it as a feature. Markets with some regulation, like tax advisers or estate agents, may be safer places to bring agents.

### Code still matters where trust matters

Unai suggested agents could use Codiluce, my tool to see how code fits together, to review PRs. That is already solved; Codiluce is for the person who wants to understand what the agents did. We both admitted we have mostly stopped reading the code of our own projects. His point: in sensitive areas like security it still matters, because you have to be able to trust it.

## What I take from it

- **GEO is work you can do in the background.** I treated appearing in AI answers as luck. Unai treats it like SEO: questions asked every day, citations read, gaps filled with content an agent drafts and he approves. I want to know what ChatGPT and Claude answer when someone asks how to become a product engineer, and whether this site is in the answer.
- **Restraint, also in how many products I build.** Unai's point was about features: when building is cheap, nothing forces you to prioritise. It works one level up too. I have several side projects, and agents make it easy to start another one. The cut list should have products on it as well.
- **Automate the loops around the product.** Errors, support emails and content drafts reach Unai's agents before they reach him. Those are the jobs that make a side project too expensive to keep running. I want the same for mine: alerts that arrive with a diagnosis, feedback that becomes a weekly report, content drafted from the gaps.
