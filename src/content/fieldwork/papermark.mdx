## Who and what

[Marc Seitz](https://mfts.io) studied physics, still writes about dark energy, and taught himself software. Before Papermark he co-founded Hackerbay as CTO, worked as CTO at the venture fund AQVC and, as Nathan Latka introduced him, helped launch Intel Ignite in Europe. In May 2023 he started an open-source side project in Munich. Today he runs it with his wife and co-founder, [Iuliia Shnai](https://x.com/shnai0), and it makes $3 million a year in recurring revenue. They split it 50/50 and have not raised a cent.

**[Papermark](https://www.papermark.com) is an open-source platform for sharing documents and running virtual data rooms, with page-by-page analytics on who read what.** You upload a pitch deck or a whole due diligence folder, share a link on your own domain, and set what the visitor must do first: verify an email, accept an NDA. Then you see who opened which page and for how long. It started as the [open-source DocSend alternative](https://www.papermark.com/docsend-alternatives); founders use it to raise, and M&A advisors, funds and banks to run deals. It runs on [papermark.com](https://www.papermark.com) or your own servers, from the [code on GitHub](https://github.com/papermark/papermark), and agents use it through an [MCP server](https://www.papermark.com/agents).

I did not talk to Marc. This piece comes from his conversation with Nathan Latka on [Top Founders](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=NKEEbYj2i6o), out on 10 November, plus an earlier [Starter Story episode](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=F8i0kkrQ8_o) with both founders, Papermark's [milestone posts](https://www.papermark.com/news/papermark-reaches-3m-arr), Marc's [blog](https://mfts.io) and the code. Like [Zernio](/in-practice/zernio/), it began with a weekend build; unlike Zernio, its code is public, so I could read it. I grouped it by territory and added what I would ask and test.

## Discovery: a tweet before the code

Marc wanted to build something open source, and he saw nobody building an open alternative to DocSend or the data room tools. Iuliia set the rule.

<Quote
  text="Don't just build something, no one's going to use it. Test it on Twitter first. If people like it, if they share it, comment, then you can build it over the weekend."
  who="Iuliia Shnai, as Marc told it"
  context="On Top Founders, about the start of Papermark"
/>

On 23 May 2023 [he bet her](https://mfts.io/blog/from-tweet-to-launch-open-source-journey) the tweet would get 50 likes. It got 265. He built the first version that weekend; the launch tweet got 450 likes and over 95,000 views. (Latka's intro gave those numbers to the first tweet. Marc's own post gives them to the launch.) Soon after, "the first customers started saying: can we pay you money?" Iuliia told [Horizons](https://horizonspod.substack.com/p/frustrated-customers-might-be-your) they paid even though the payment flow was not finished.

Why DocSend? "Everyone knew DocSend and everyone didn't like it." Dropbox bought it during the COVID boom and, in Marc's view, it stopped improving. DocSend later dropped its free plan, "an opening for us". The timing was a wave too. In 2023 commercial open source was rebuilding older SaaS products: Cal.com for Calendly, Documenso for DocuSign. Papermark's [OSS Friends page](https://www.papermark.com/oss-friends) still lists them.

That was the first customer. By 2026 it was another: most new customers come for a data room for fundraising, M&A or due diligence. The home page now lists customers by assets under management.

## Strategy: open core, feature parity, then a different buyer

The model is open core: an AGPLv3 core anyone can self-host, a hosted service, and an enterprise licence for the features in the repository's `ee/` folders. On Starter Story, Marc gave four reasons it works: nobody can charge less than a free core, anyone can try it, the community adds R&D, and a bank can audit the code instead of asking for access.

Open source got them attention; revenue waited for feature parity. The first $1M ARR took until September 2025, because "no one wants a half-baked product" and Papermark was "missing maybe one or two features" buyers expected. **Once it matched the incumbents at a lower price, "it became a no-brainer".**

<Quote
  text="You need to reach at least feature parity with the existing tools in the market, and then you need to out-ship them. You want to become the clear successor to the incumbents."
  who="Marc Seitz"
  context="On Starter Story, his advice for open-source alternatives"
/>

Where Marc chose to differ is the brand: a custom domain out of the box, and rooms that look like the customer, where "the incumbents put their own brand front and center".

Pricing is flat, with unlimited visitors. Business includes three users for €59 a month, billed yearly; Papermark's [own comparison](https://www.papermark.com/blog/papermark-vs-docsend) prices DocSend's closest plan at $45 per user (a competitor's numbers: DocSend's pricing page would not open for me). The amounts are also in the public code, in `ee/stripe/utils.ts`.

## Distribution: every shared link is an ad

The first thousand GitHub stars came "within the first couple of months", from viral posts, Hacktoberfest and a circle of commercial open-source founders: "we had each other's back". A thousand was Marc's own validation; the repository has 9,382 today.

The channels changed with the buyer: "None of our customers today are on Twitter itself. Most of them are on LinkedIn." Iuliia told Horizons they tried more than 14 channels and kept three, without saying which.

## Activation: badges first, a room in a minute

The first thing the home page says is not what Papermark does. It says "ISO 27001 certified · SOC 2 Type II", then the headline, then "Trusted by 80,000+ global companies", logos and a 4.9 rating. For someone about to upload a company's financials, that is the order of questions.

The second job is setup time. You start from a template for fundraising, M&A or LP reporting, or describe the deal and AI proposes the folders, so "the first hour goes into the deal, not into folders". Data room plans start with a 7-day trial.

## Retention: deals end, so the plan can pause

Neither episode touched churn. Data rooms are a hard case: the need lasts as long as a fundraise or a sale. A founder raises every year or two. An M&A advisor runs deals all year.

The code shows how Papermark handles it. The cancellation flow offers a pause: "Take a break for 3 months and resume when you're ready. You pay $0 for 3 months. All your links continue to work." Three days before billing resumes, an email says so. And if someone opens a paused team's room, the team gets an email anyway: "Your team is currently paused, so detailed visitor information is not available. To see who visited your dataroom and access full analytics, please unpause your subscription."

## Engineering: an open repo with a paid folder

Papermark is two Next.js projects in TypeScript: the marketing site, and the open-source app behind app.papermark.com. On Starter Story they named Vercel, Postgres on PlanetScale, Trigger.dev, Resend and Stripe; the code adds Tinybird for analytics events.

The open-core line is drawn in folders. The licence makes everything under `ee/` and `app/(ee)` commercial and the rest AGPLv3. Inside `ee/features` are AI, billing, permissions, security, templates and workflows: the data room features people pay for. The [about page](https://www.papermark.com/about) goes further than the licence file: "self-hosting on your own infrastructure for production or commercial use requires an enterprise license".

The heavy work runs outside the request. Jobs in `lib/trigger` turn PDFs into page images, optimise videos, build bulk downloads and send notifications, so none of it happens while a visitor waits. And protection is most of the product: dynamic watermarks, screenshot protection, NDAs before access, email verification, one-time codes for bulk downloads, SAML SSO and SCIM.

## AI: agents that open data rooms

In June 2026 Papermark shipped [Papermark Agents](https://www.papermark.com/agents): a full API, a CLI and an MCP server, "so an AI agent can create a room, upload files, set permissions and read analytics". The page lists 57 typed tools, each mapped to a REST endpoint, scoped tokens, and an append-only audit log. Install is one line, `npx -y @papermark/mcp-server`. The home page banner says Papermark is now a verified connector in Claude.

Inside the product, AI shortens the dull parts: it builds a room's folders from a description of the deal, and answers questions across documents with citations, "only from documents the token is allowed to read".

Marc was more careful about AI in the code. Outside contributions fall as a project grows, and his contribution graph showed "a lot of the code on me, and then you've got Cursor and CodeRabbit sneaking in there as well". AI holds a big code base better than a casual contributor, "to the detriment of individual contributors, unfortunately". What is left for them: translations, bug fixes, security reports.

## Operations: compliance as the product

## What I take from it

- **Open source got them attention; parity got them paid.** The repository brought stars, trust and the first customers within months. Revenue took 18 months to the first million, because buyers already knew what they wanted. When I build an alternative to something people know, I want the list of features that make buyers stay with the incumbent, written down and worked through, before I count stars.
- **Put your name on the customer's work.** Every free link on papermark.com shows the product to an investor, a buyer or a lawyer who runs deals too. Zernio grows through search and paid ads; Papermark grows through the link itself. For my own products the question is the same: what does a user send to someone else, and does it carry my name?
- **For project-shaped products, retention is a pause, not a renewal.** Deals end. Papermark's answer is in the code, not in the episodes: pause for three months, keep the links alive, and let a visitor be the reminder. I want to know how many paused teams come back, and I would design the same thing into any product used once per project.
