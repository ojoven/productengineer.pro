## Who and what

I first noticed [Mamitsu](https://usemamitsu.com/) on Twitter. Giving an agent a way to understand a video immediately made sense to me. When I spoke with its maker, Unai Esteibar, I wanted to understand the work around that capability: how he chose the product, made it dependable, and started getting it in front of people.

Mamitsu takes an Instagram, TikTok, YouTube or X link and a question, then **returns structured data**. A cooking reel can become a recipe; a travel video can become a list of places. Developers use its API, and agents can access it through MCP, the protocol for connecting agents to external tools.

What interested me most was how often Unai’s decisions crossed the boundaries of a job title. Our conversation moved between product judgment, engineering, operations and distribution. I have grouped it by those territories below.

## On the map

## Discovery: the useful part inside another idea

<Skills label="Skills discussed" ids={['pain-points', 'competitive-analysis']} />

Mamitsu began inside an app for making better coffee at home. Unai described starting with a recipe, tasting the result, and adjusting the next brew. Even translating grinder settings between machines became a substantial problem.

Then other makers wanted part of what he was building: one for family recipes, another for a coffee app, another for travel. **The reusable opportunity was extracting useful information from content that was difficult to work with.** Unai parked the larger idea and gave himself two weeks to package that capability as a product.

I asked about alternatives and potential paying customers. He had investigated services including Supadata and Apify, and saw an opening to offer extraction at a much lower price. That was his bet. Requests from peers and activity around competing products gave him reasons to try; we did not establish repeat demand or willingness to pay for Mamitsu.

## Strategy: deciding what Mamitsu will leave out

<Skills label="Skills discussed" ids={['value-proposition', 'differentiation', 'scope-cutting']} />

When I asked what he had deliberately cut, Unai named a general-purpose scraper and features built around YouTube transcripts. His bet was that giving the model the original content would leave it more freedom to extract what the caller actually needed.

He also described a tempting extension: someone could send a recipe to an app’s Instagram account and have it appear in their recipe library. He thought it was feasible, but had not validated enough demand to justify building it. For now, he preferred to **finish packaging and distributing the core service**.

## AI: a link and a question become usable data

<Skills label="Skills discussed" ids={['structured-outputs', 'mcp', 'ai-evals']} />

The product’s contract is straightforward: **send a link and describe what you want back**. Mamitsu’s [public explanation](https://usemamitsu.com/) says it watches the video, listens to the audio and reads the caption. A JSON Schema can specify the output fields when an application needs a consistent shape.

That matters because the useful detail might appear briefly on screen or be spoken without appearing in the caption. **A transcript alone can miss part of the answer.** An agent can request the details needed for its task and then use them elsewhere, such as in a recipe collection or a travel plan.

Quality is the next part of that contract. Unai wanted to compare extractions with a larger model to investigate whether the results were reasonable. That was still an evaluation idea. I would also want to check the answers against the source content.

## Engineering: earning the confidence to sell it

<Skills label="Skills discussed" ids={['throughput', 'scalability']} />

Unai was interested in the distance between a working prototype and something he felt confident selling. He said early fetching attempts could trigger bans after only a few requests. By the interview, he reported testing 3,000 TikTok requests in a minute, following repeated load testing.

I read that as evidence of the work he was putting into reliability. We did not inspect the test conditions, success rates or production traffic, so it is not an independently verified capacity figure. His point was practical: **the service needed to withstand use beyond his own demo**.

## Shipping: let risk decide who approves

<Skills label="Skills discussed" ids={['delivery-risk', 'rollbacks', 'launch-telemetry']} />

Unai described a workflow that starts with a discussion of the feature and a rough technical approach. Before delegating implementation, he considers the blast radius, sensitive changes such as database migrations, and how a rollback would work.

Hermes coordinates the work, with Pi agents handling code changes. The implementation comes back with verification evidence; another agent reviews the pull request and assesses whether it can proceed. **Higher-risk work needs Unai’s approval.** Lower-risk, reversible changes can move through automatically.

The agent then watches the rollout according to its plan, checking logs and requests and rolling back if necessary. Unai said a rollback had happened once. What caught my attention was the explicit decision about where human attention belongs.

## Operations: give every problem somewhere to go

<Skills label="Skills discussed" ids={['alerting', 'issue-investigation', 'support-workflows']} />

When I asked how he collected feedback, Unai’s first answer was simple: a support email address. Behind it, he described a more developed operational workflow.

An agent polls his error tracker and opens a Slack thread when something new appears. It investigates whether the issue needs attention and, when it identifies a fix, can start the pull request process. Support emails can be forwarded to an internal address and enter a similar investigation flow. Unai joins the thread when needed.

He told me he had discovered one fix only after it was already in production. For him, **automating the maintenance around a product changes which small products are viable to run**. That is a substantial part of Mamitsu’s story: the work continues after extraction succeeds.

## Distribution: be findable when an agent does the research

<Skills label="Skills discussed" ids={['seo', 'content-marketing', 'attribution']} />

Unai showed me how he was monitoring Mamitsu’s appearance in AI answers using Omnia. He cared especially about **questions that did not mention Mamitsu by name**: those were opportunities for someone to discover it while researching a problem.

He described better visibility for some specific requests, such as extracting recipes or returning JSON, and gaps around broader wording. Those gaps informed guides and documentation. When I asked whether generated content went straight onto the site, he said he generally read it before publication.

There was a useful limit to the story. Unai said Mamitsu was appearing more often in the answers he monitored, but could not reliably attribute new users to those appearances. I would keep those questions separate: **being cited, being visited and being used are different outcomes**.

## Measurement: learn what people are extracting

<Skills label="Skills discussed" ids={['usage-observation', 'feature-usage']} />

I asked whether he was learning from the prompts and extractions people submitted. At the time, Unai was using Rybbit for website analytics and occasionally checking product activity by hand. He had not established an automated usage analysis or extraction evaluation process.

Near the end, I asked what he might try after our conversation. He returned to that question: **a weekly review of extractions could reveal use cases he had not anticipated**. He also wanted to compare results with a larger model as a way to investigate quality.

## Beyond the product

<Skills label="Wider discussion" ids={['tradeoffs', 'seeking-feedback', 'professional-development']} />

Three threads stayed with me beyond Mamitsu itself.

**Restraint becomes a more deliberate skill.** Unai argued that limited engineering capacity used to force prioritisation. When building gets easier, that constraint weakens. He admired products where you could feel that someone had carefully chosen what to include. I recognised the temptation to keep adding things in my own projects.

**A familiar interface can be part of the value.** We compared working with agents through Slack and WhatsApp, and the effort involved in getting someone to adopt another application. I brought up that tension in my own Basque-learning product: it competes with places people already open habitually. We treated this as an open product question, rather than a prediction that every product should become a chat.

**A demo makes an idea easier to discuss.** Unai described building a small demonstration in a couple of hours instead of struggling to explain an idea abstractly. We talked about the peers we share projects with, and why those conversations matter. For me, this also connected to the product engineer role: deciding what matters, making something concrete, and finding out whether it helps.

## What I take from it

I came in interested in giving agents access to video. I left thinking about the decisions that make that capability worth offering: a clear boundary, confidence under load, a release process that accounts for risk, and a way to learn from actual use.

My strongest takeaway is the question I would bring back to Unai next time: **what did people repeatedly use Mamitsu to accomplish that he had not expected?** The product and its operational machinery are already concrete. A regular review of those tasks could give him better evidence for the next thing to build—or to leave out.
