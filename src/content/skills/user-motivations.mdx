## Why it matters for a product engineer

Two people can do the same thing for different reasons, and the reason decides which change they will
welcome. A goal tells you what to make faster. A fear tells you what never to add, such as a score for
someone who already feels they are failing. An engineer who knows both makes better small decisions
every day: the defaults, the copy, what to show first.

## How to do it

1. Separate three kinds of goal: functional (the task), emotional (how the person wants to feel) and
   social (how they want to look to other people).
2. Ask about a recent real moment, not about wishes. What happened just before? What were they afraid
   would go wrong?
3. Listen for the forces behind a switch: the push of the current problem, the pull of the new
   solution, and the anxiety and habits that hold people back.
4. Write each motivation with what it asks of the product: a limit, a default, or a thing to avoid.
5. Remember that motivation alone is not enough. In BJ Fogg's model, a behaviour happens when
   motivation, ability and a prompt meet at the same moment. When motivation is low, make the action
   easier.
6. When people say one thing and do another, trust what they do, and look for the motivation that
   explains it.

## Common mistakes

- Listing only functional goals, because they are the easiest to measure.
- Taking stated wishes as motivation: "I would use it every day."
- Treating motivation as fixed. The same person can want something badly at 3 a.m. and not at all at
  noon.
- Trying to raise motivation with copy when the real problem is that the action is hard.

## Example

An agent's audit of RelievApp named three goals for someone in an anxious moment. The emotional goal
became a rule for every session: one gesture, rhythmic, forgiving, impossible to fail. The social
goal pointed to a claim the product hardly used: it is silent and works with one hand.
