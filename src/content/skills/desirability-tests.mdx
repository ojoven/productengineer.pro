## Why it matters for a product engineer

Most products fail because nobody wants them, not because the code is bad. A desirability test checks
demand before you spend weeks building. An engineer can often run one in a day, with a page, a post or
a button for a feature that does not exist yet.

## How to do it

1. Write the riskiest assumption, such as "founders want to see who read their deck", and the action
   that would show it is true.
2. Choose a test where people give something up: time, an email address, a pre-order, a click on a
   feature that is not built yet.
3. Set the bar before you start: the number that means "build it".
4. Show the test to the people it is for, where they already are.
5. Stop at a fixed date and decide: build, change the offer, or stop.
6. Talk to the people who said yes. Their words become your copy.

## Common mistakes

- Asking "Would you use this?". People are kind, and the answer costs them nothing.
- No bar set in advance, so any result looks like a success.
- Treating likes as purchases. A like is the weakest signal; money is the strongest.
- A fake button with no explanation afterwards. Tell people the feature is not there yet.
- Testing demand and usability at the same time.

## Example

Iuliia Shnai gave Marc Seitz a rule: test the idea on Twitter first, and build only if people react.
Marc bet her that a tweet about an open-source alternative to DocSend would get 50 likes. It got 265.
He built the first version that weekend, and soon the first customers asked if they could pay.
