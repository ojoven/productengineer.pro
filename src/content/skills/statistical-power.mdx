## Why it matters for a product engineer

Power is the chance that a test finds an effect that is really there. A test with low power usually
says "no difference", even for a good change. When it does find something, the effect it reports is
often too large. Teams then drop good ideas and believe lucky ones. Planning for power is part of
deciding whether a test is worth running.

## How to do it

1. Aim for at least 80% power for the smallest effect that would change your decision.
2. Raise power with more users, a bigger change, or a metric with less noise.
3. Use a metric close to the change. A button test moves clicks more clearly than revenue.
4. Reduce the number of variants when traffic is low.
5. Read a "no difference" result from a low-power test as "we do not know", not as "it does not
   work".
6. Be suspicious of large effects from small tests. They are often too good to be true.

## Common mistakes

- Running a test with whatever traffic arrives, and never checking the power.
- Treating "not significant" as proof that there is no effect.
- Believing the size of an effect from an underpowered test.
- Splitting a small audience into many groups.

## Example

RelievApp chose a broad catalog of 21 sessions on purpose, as research. Its maker wrote down the price
of that choice: with little traffic, 21 sessions split the sample 21 ways. Each session gets a small
share of the people, so it takes longer to tell a good session from a lucky one.
