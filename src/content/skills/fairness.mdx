## Why it matters for a product engineer

Rules, models and defaults can treat groups of people differently even when nobody intends it. A
ranking model learns from past behaviour; a fraud rule matches one group's habits more than another's;
a default suits the people who designed it. Product engineers build and ship these systems, so they
are in a good position to test them. Fairness matters most where the product decides about access,
price, visibility or opportunity.

## How to do it

1. List the decisions your product makes about people, and who could be treated unfairly by each one.
2. Measure outcomes by group, not only overall: error rates, approval rates, who is shown.
3. Test with data that represents the people who will use the product.
4. Ask whether the decision needs to be automatic at all, or whether users could choose.
5. Give people a way to report a problem, and review those reports.

## Common mistakes

- Checking only the average, which hides differences between groups.
- Assuming a model is fair because it does not use sensitive attributes.
- Treating a fairness metric as the whole answer.
- Testing once at launch and never again.

## Example

In 2020, Twitter users raised concerns that automatic image cropping favoured lighter-skinned people
over darker-skinned people. Twitter's researchers tested it: for each pair of groups, they combined
photos 10,000 times and recorded which face the crop chose. A fair crop would choose each group 50% of
the time. They found that women were chosen 58% of the time and white people 54%. They also argued that
numbers alone could not capture the harm. Twitter removed the automatic crop and, in May 2021, released a design
close to showing photos with no crop at all.
