## Why it matters for a product engineer

A feature that nobody finds has the same value as a feature that was never built, but it still costs
maintenance. Low use can mean two different things: people do not need the feature, or people do not
know it exists. The fixes are very different. One is to change or remove the feature. The other is to
change where and when people see it. Product engineers can measure the difference, because they
control the events and the placement.

## How to do it

1. Before launch, define who should use the feature and what "adopted" means, for example used twice
   in 30 days.
2. Track three steps separately: saw the entry point, tried the feature, came back to it.
3. Put the entry point where the need appears, not only in a menu or a release note.
4. Announce the change in the product and in a changelog written for people, not as a list of commits.
5. Review adoption after a few weeks, and decide: improve discovery, improve the feature, or stop.

## Common mistakes

- Judging a feature by total clicks, without knowing how many people saw it.
- Launch emails as the only way people learn about a feature.
- Hiding new features behind a menu icon to keep the screen clean.
- Tooltips on every screen, which people learn to close without reading.

## Example

In 2016, the Nielsen Norman Group tested six websites with 179 participants. The tasks did not tell
people to use the menu. On desktop, people used a hidden menu, such as a hamburger icon, in 27% of
cases. When the same kind of menu was visible, they used it in 48% of cases. When part of it was
visible, they used it in 50%. Some participants even said that sites with hidden navigation had no menu.
