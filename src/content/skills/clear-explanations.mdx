## Why it matters for a product engineer

Product engineers explain technical things to people who decide: a risk to a manager, a trade-off to
a designer, a limit to a customer. If the explanation is unclear, the decision is made on a guess. A
clear explanation starts from what the listener already knows, uses an example they can picture, and
keeps the numbers that matter. It is simple, but not less accurate.

## How to do it

1. Ask what the listener needs to decide, and explain only what that decision needs.
2. Start from something they already know, and connect the new idea to it.
3. Turn abstract numbers into a picture: a day, a user, a cost they recognise.
4. Use one concrete example before any general rule.
5. Check understanding by asking them to explain it back, or to make the decision.

## Common mistakes

- Starting with how it works instead of why it matters to the listener.
- Jargon and acronyms the listener does not share.
- Numbers without a comparison, so nobody can tell if they are big or small.
- Simplifying so much that the explanation becomes wrong.

## Example

After the Challenger disaster in 1986, Richard Feynman asked how likely NASA thought a Shuttle loss
was. Estimates ranged from roughly 1 in 100, from working engineers, to 1 in 100,000, from management.
He explained what the low number meant: you could launch a Shuttle every day for 300 years and expect
to lose only one. Put that way, anyone could question the number without any statistics.
