## Why it matters for a product engineer

A list of features does not explain why a product should exist. A narrative does: who has the
problem, what they do today, what changes for them, and how we will know it worked. Writing that
story in full sentences exposes gaps that slides hide. It also helps a team keep the same picture of
the product while many people build different parts of it. Product engineers who can write the story
of a feature can also defend it, cut it, or change it for the right reasons.

## How to do it

1. Start from the customer: who they are and what problem they have today.
2. Describe the experience after the change, in the customer's words, before any technical detail.
3. Answer the hard questions in writing: cost, risks, what we will not do.
4. Keep it short enough to read in the meeting, and let people read before they discuss.
5. Revise it as you learn, and keep the latest version where everyone can find it.

## Common mistakes

- Starting with the technology instead of the customer.
- Long documents that hide the main point on page six.
- Stories about an imagined user instead of a real one.
- Writing the narrative after the decision, to justify it.

## Example

Since 2004, most of Amazon's major products have started with a PR/FAQ: a press release and a list of
frequently asked questions, written before anything is built. The press release must be less than one
page, and the FAQ five pages or less. A common question from executives is "so what?" If the press
release does not describe something better than what exists, it is not worth building. Most PR/FAQs
never become products.
