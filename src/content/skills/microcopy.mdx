## Why it matters for a product engineer

Microcopy is the text people read while they do something: a button label, a hint, an error. Engineers
write much of it, often as a placeholder that ships. People scan this text in a second and act on it,
so one word can change what they do. It is also cheap to change and easy to test.

## How to do it

1. Name the action on the button with a verb and its object: "Save draft", not "OK".
2. Put the important words first and remove the rest. "Total cost", not "This is the total cost".
3. Use the words your users use, the same word for the same thing on every screen.
4. In errors, say what happened and how to fix it. Keep "sorry" for serious failures.
5. Add help text only when research shows people need it. If the interface needs an explanation, fix
   the interface.
6. Read every string in place, at phone width, before release.

## Common mistakes

- Placeholder text, such as "Submit" or "Error 500", that nobody replaces.
- Jokes in error messages. People often meet errors when they are stressed.
- Instructions that rely on colour or position: "click the green button".
- Link text such as "click here", which means nothing when a screen reader lists the links.

## Example

On a large online shop, the checkout started with a login form: two fields and the buttons "Login" and
"Register". In usability tests, first-time buyers resented registering. The team replaced "Register"
with "Continue" and one sentence: you do not need an account to buy. Jared Spool reports that the
number of customers who bought went up by 45%, with $300 million more revenue in the first year.
