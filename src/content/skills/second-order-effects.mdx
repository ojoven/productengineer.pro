## Why it matters for a product engineer

The first effect of a change is usually the one you planned. The next effects come from how people
react: users and creators adapt, support load moves, a metric becomes a target and stops meaning what
it meant. These effects often take weeks to appear and land on other teams. Asking "and then what?"
two or three times before a launch finds many of them. Guardrail metrics catch some of the rest.

## How to do it

1. Write the intended effect of the change in one sentence.
2. Ask "and then what?" for each group affected: users, creators, support, sales, the system itself.
3. Ask how people could reach the new target without reaching the goal behind it.
4. Choose guardrail metrics that would show the bad effects, and watch them after launch.
5. Review the change again after a few weeks, when people have adapted.

## Common mistakes

- Judging a change only by the metric it was designed to move.
- Rewarding a number that is easy to inflate.
- Stopping the review at launch day.
- Assuming people will use the product exactly as designed.

## Example

YouTube's recommendations show a chain of second-order effects. In 2008, the system ranked videos by
popularity. By 2011, YouTube learned that a click did not mean someone watched the video: titles and
thumbnails could promise what the video did not show. In 2012 it added watch time. In 2015 it noticed
sensational tabloid content on homepages and demoted it. In 2019 it began demoting borderline content,
which cut watch time of that content from recommendations by 70% in the US.
