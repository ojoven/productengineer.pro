## Why it matters for a product engineer

At Bing, less than a third of the ideas tested moved the metrics they were designed to improve. So
most results call for a change or a stop, not a celebration. Engineers live with every feature that
stays: its code, its bugs and its support. Deciding honestly what to do with a result keeps the
product, and the codebase, from filling up with things that do not work.

## How to do it

1. Before the release, write the success criterion, the guardrails and what result would mean stop.
2. When the result comes in, compare it with those criteria, not with what you hoped.
3. Iterate when the idea works in part: one group responds, or a guardrail fails for a reason you can
   fix.
4. Expand when the criteria are met: roll out to more users, more platforms, or the next step.
5. Stop when the criteria are missed with enough data. Remove the code, not only the button.
6. Write the decision and the reason where the team can find it later.

## Common mistakes

- Choosing the criteria after seeing the result.
- Iterating forever on a feature that never meets its goal.
- Expanding because a feature has fans, while the metric did not move.
- Hiding a feature instead of removing it, so its cost stays.

## Example

Bing tested links to parts of an advertiser's site under its ads. The rule was set in advance: more
revenue per user, without hurting engagement. The first version failed it, so the team iterated
instead of shipping. RelievApp built a broad catalog of 21 sessions on purpose, so that the numbers
can say which ones to keep, change or drop. ([RelievApp in In practice](/in-practice/relievapp-part-1/))
