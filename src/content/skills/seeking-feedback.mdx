## Why it matters for a product engineer

Product engineers work across code, design, data and people, so their blind spots are spread over
many areas. Other people see those blind spots more clearly than you do. Most of them will not tell
you unless you ask, and ask in a way that makes honesty safe. Asked for often, feedback is small and
easy to act on. Asked for rarely, it arrives late, as a surprise, in a performance review.

## How to do it

1. Ask specific questions: "What would have made this design doc clearer?" works better than "Any feedback?"
2. Ask soon after the work, while people still remember the details.
3. Listen without defending yourself; ask questions only to understand.
4. Thank the person, even when the feedback is hard to hear.
5. Act on it where it makes sense, and tell them what you changed.

## Common mistakes

- Asking only people who are likely to agree with you.
- Explaining why the feedback is wrong instead of trying to understand it.
- Asking for feedback and then doing nothing visible with it.
- Waiting for formal reviews instead of asking every week.

## Example

Kim Scott tells this story from her first months at Google. After a presentation that went well, her
boss, Sheryl Sandberg, pointed out that Scott had said "um" a lot, and suggested that Google could
hire a speaking coach.
Scott brushed the comment away. Sandberg tried again, then said it plainly. Only then did Scott listen,
work with the coach and lose the habit. Scott later suggested a simple way to ask for feedback: put her
praise-and-criticism chart near your desk, and ask people to mark where your last conversation with them
fell.
