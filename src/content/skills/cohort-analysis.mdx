## Why it matters for a product engineer

Total numbers hide what happens to people. A product can grow every month while most new users leave,
because more keep arriving. Cohort analysis follows groups that started at the same time, such as
everyone who signed up in March, so you can see whether newer users stay better than older ones. That
is how you see whether the changes you ship help.

## How to do it

1. Choose the cohort: usually the week or month of sign-up, or of the first payment.
2. Choose what counts as still there: an action that shows value, or revenue, not a login.
3. Build the grid: one row per cohort, one column per period since the start.
4. Read down the columns: are newer cohorts higher than older ones at the same age?
5. Mark releases, price changes and new channels on the grid, so you can connect them to changes.
6. Split cohorts by channel, plan or first action when the grid raises a question.

## Common mistakes

- Cohorts so broad, such as a whole year, that changes disappear.
- Reading the latest cohorts before they are old enough.
- Mixing users from very different channels in one cohort.
- One average retention number instead of the grid.

## Example

Andreessen Horowitz collected revenue retention from dozens of AI companies, by cohort, and found
three phases. In the first three months, many "AI tourists" try the product and leave. From about
month 3 the curve becomes flat, with the users who found a real use. After six to twelve months, the
customers who stayed often expand. So a16z reads a cohort from month 3, not from month 0.
