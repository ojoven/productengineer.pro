## Why it matters for a product engineer

An average describes nobody. A change can help mobile users and hurt desktop users, and the total can
show no effect. Splitting results by the way people use the product often explains numbers that made
no sense. Engineers own the events and properties that make a split possible, so they decide whether
the question can be answered at all.

## How to do it

1. Before you look at a result, choose a few segments that could plausibly behave differently: plan,
   platform, channel, the first action, or what people use the product for.
2. Prefer segments based on behaviour over demographics. What someone does in the first week often
   says more than their country or job title.
3. Record what you need at the source, as event properties, so you do not have to rebuild it later.
4. Compare each segment's share of users with its share of the outcome: revenue, retention or
   support load.
5. Treat a surprise in one segment as a new hypothesis. Test it, rather than shipping because of it.

## Common mistakes

- Splitting until something looks significant. With enough segments, one always will.
- Segments too small to say anything.
- Deciding from one segment's win and ignoring the total.
- Never splitting at all, and running the product on blended numbers.

## Example

Zernio, an API for posting to social networks, grew to about €5M of annual recurring revenue in 13
months without splitting its users. The weekend before a podcast recording, its founder split them by
use case for the first time. Developers who build Zernio into their own products were about 2% of
signups and half the monthly revenue, and they churned far less. ([Zernio in In practice](/in-practice/zernio/))
