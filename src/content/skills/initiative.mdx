## Why it matters for a product engineer

Many problems in a product are in nobody's tickets: a slow page nobody owns, a confusing flow, an idea
nobody has tried. Initiative means noticing them and acting, either by doing the work or by getting
the right person to do it. It is the difference between owning a product and executing tickets. It
works best inside an agreed direction, and with evidence. An engineer who acts without telling anyone,
or against the team's goals, creates new problems instead of solving them.

## How to do it

1. Look for problems that matter to the goals and that nobody else is working on.
2. Make a small, cheap version first: a prototype, a script, a test.
3. Tell the people affected what you are doing, and why.
4. Let evidence decide, not opinion: measure the result with real users.
5. Finish what you start, or hand it over clearly.

## Common mistakes

- Starting many things and finishing few.
- Choosing work because it is visible, not because it matters.
- Surprising your team or manager with work they did not expect.
- Waiting for permission for small changes that are easy to undo.

## Example

In 2006, Greg Linden wrote about his early years at Amazon. He built a prototype that showed
recommendations in the shopping cart. A marketing senior vice-president was against it, worried that
it would distract people from checking out, and Linden was told not to work on it any more. He
prepared an online test instead. The feature won by such a wide margin that not having it live was
costing Amazon money, and it launched. Linden adds a warning: in some organizations, challenging a
senior executive like this would be a fatal mistake.
