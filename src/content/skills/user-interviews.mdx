## Why it matters for a product engineer

Engineers make many small product decisions every day: which edge case matters, which step to cut,
what to call a button. Interviews give those decisions a real person behind them. They also show the
need behind a feature request, which is often different from the feature that was asked for.

## How to do it

1. Choose one decision the interviews should inform. Then find five people who did the thing
   recently.
2. Ask about the last time it happened: "Tell me about the last time you sent a deck to investors.
   What did you do first?"
3. Follow the story with "why?" and "what happened next?". Do not pitch your idea, and do not ask if
   they would use it.
4. Listen more than you talk. Write down what they did, which tools they used and what it cost them,
   in their words.
5. Ask users who contact support or request a feature for a short call. They already have a story to
   tell.
6. Put a fixed slot in your calendar, such as one call a week, so interviews do not stop when a
   deadline comes.

## Common mistakes

- Asking "Would you use this?". People say yes to be nice, and a hypothetical yes costs them nothing.
- Treating a feature request as the answer. Ask what they were trying to do when they wanted it.
- Talking only to friends and happy customers.
- Interrupting with your solution before the story is finished.
- Asking people to predict what they will do, instead of what they did.

## Example

ThoughtFlow, a mind-mapping add-on for monday.com, built the most requested features, such as
keyboard navigation, but few trial users upgraded. Its founder and technical lead, Kranthi Kiran,
started to invite users who contacted support to an interview about their team, projects and
workflows. A pattern appeared: most users did not want to create mind maps, they wanted to see what
was already on their boards. The team built Bird's Eye View, an app that draws the mind map from
existing boards.
