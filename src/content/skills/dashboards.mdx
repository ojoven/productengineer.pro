## Why it matters for a product engineer

A dashboard is useful when it answers one question fast: is the service healthy, did the release work,
are users completing the main task? Teams often build many dashboards, and then nobody knows which one
to open during an incident. Product engineers can put product metrics next to system metrics, so a fall
in signups and a rise in errors appear on the same screen.

## How to do it

1. Write the question the dashboard answers in its title or first line.
2. Put the most important graphs at the top: what customers experience, such as availability and
   latency.
3. Show the time zone, use the same time range on every graph, and avoid horizontal scrolling.
4. Link each high-level graph to the more detailed dashboard behind it.
5. Use alarms to detect problems, and dashboards to investigate them. Review dashboards in a regular
   meeting, and delete the ones nobody uses.

## Common mistakes

- Dashboards that only engineers can read, with no product metrics.
- One huge dashboard with every metric the service emits.
- Graphs with different time ranges or time zones on the same screen.
- Expecting people to notice problems by watching dashboards.

## Example

Amazon explains how it builds dashboards in its Builders' Library. The most important dashboards show
customer experience, with overall availability and end-to-end latency at the top. Graphs fit the
smallest expected screen, because an on-call engineer on a laptop at 3 AM may not see a horizontal
scroll bar. Shared dashboards default to UTC. Amazon also notes that most of the time nobody watches
dashboards, so automated alarms detect problems, and dashboards help find the cause.
