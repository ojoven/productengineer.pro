## Why it matters for a product engineer

A reminder can bring someone back at the moment they need the product: a payment is due, a delivery
arrives, an appointment is close. Each message also costs attention. With too many messages, or the
wrong ones, people turn notifications off, and then the useful ones cannot reach them either. Product
engineers build the triggers, the timing and the settings, so they decide much of whether reminders
help or annoy.

## How to do it

1. Start from an event that matters to the user, such as a due date or a change they asked to follow.
2. Send at a useful time: early enough to act, in the user's time zone, and once for each event.
3. Put what happened and what to do in the first words, because many people read only the preview.
4. Ask for push permission when the value is clear, and let people choose which types they get.
5. Measure the outcome the reminder is for, along with opt-outs. Test the wording as well as the
   timing.

## Common mistakes

- Asking for permission on the first screen, before the user knows why.
- Promotions sent through the channel people allowed for important updates.
- One setting for all notifications, so turning off one type turns off everything.
- Counting clicks while opt-outs rise.

## Example

A London NHS hospital trust sent outpatients a text reminder five days before their appointment.
Researchers tested new wordings against the existing message in a randomized trial with 10,111
patients. A message that stated what a missed appointment costs the health service cut the rate of
missed appointments from 11.1% to 8.4%. A second trial repeated the result. The team estimated about
5,800 fewer missed appointments a year in that trust, at no extra cost.
