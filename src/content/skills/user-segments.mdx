## Why it matters for a product engineer

An average describes nobody. When a metric moves, or a feature works for some people and fails for
others, segments explain why. For a product engineer they decide who a change is really for, and how
to read its result.

## How to do it

1. Start from a decision, such as what to build next or why retention fell. The decision tells you
   which differences matter.
2. Group people by need or behaviour: the job they hire the product for, how often they use it, their
   plan, their first action. Demographics rarely explain behaviour.
3. Check each segment in the data: how many people, how long they stay, how much they pay.
4. Keep three to five segments with names the whole team uses.
5. Split the key metrics by segment before you draw a conclusion. A change can help one group and
   hurt another, and the average hides both.
6. Make sure the product can tell segments apart, for example with a property on the user or an
   event. See [behavioral segmentation](/skills-map/measurement/behavioral-analysis/behavioral-segmentation/).

## Common mistakes

- Segments by age or country that predict nothing about use.
- So many small segments that none has enough people to read.
- Segments that exist in a slide but cannot be found in the data.
- Building for the loudest segment instead of the most valuable one.
- Never checking again after the product changes.

## Example

Mamitsu's beta testers seemed to be three groups: one friend wanted the video import for family
recipes, another for his own coffee app, a third for a travel app. Grouped by topic, that is three
small markets. Grouped by need, it is one: developers who need structured data out of videos. That
segment became Mamitsu's user, and coffee drinkers stopped being the target.
