## Why it matters for a product engineer

Not all evidence is equal. Someone who pays is stronger evidence than someone who says they would pay.
What people do is stronger than what they answer in a survey. Engineers build the instruments that
create much of this evidence, so they decide whether it can be trusted: what is logged, what is a
default, and what is really the user's choice.

## How to do it

1. Label every claim with where it comes from: measured in the product, observed, reported by
   someone, or assumed.
2. Rank commitments above words. In The Mom Test, a commitment means giving up something of value:
   time, reputation or money.
3. Treat compliments, general claims ("I always"), future promises ("I would") and ideas as weak.
4. Ask about specific past events, not opinions about the future.
5. Check how a number was produced before you trust it: who chose the value, and was there a default?
6. Match the evidence to the size of the bet. A month of work needs more than a few warm
   conversations.

## Common mistakes

- Counting "I would definitely buy that" as demand.
- Many weak signals added together and treated as one strong one.
- A metric that measures the product's own default, not the user's choice.
- Throwing away a strong result because it disagrees with the plan.

## Example

An agent's audit of RelievApp found that the rating after a session started 30% better than the rating
before it. The agent saved "3 points lighter" without touching the slider. Any average built on those
ratings measured the default, not the session. The fix is now built: no head start, and a rating
the person did not touch does not count.
