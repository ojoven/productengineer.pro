## Why it matters for a product engineer

Product data is full of patterns that look like causes. Usage went up after a launch; churn fell
after a price change. Causal reasoning asks what else could explain the pattern, and what would have
happened without the change. A product engineer who asks this avoids giving credit, or blame, to the
wrong work.

## How to do it

1. Write the claim as cause and effect, then list the other explanations: seasons, campaigns,
   changes in the user mix, bugs, and cause and effect the other way round.
2. If you can, run a randomized experiment.
3. If you cannot, find a comparison group that did not get the change but followed a similar trend
   before it.
4. Use difference-in-differences: compare the change in the group that got the change with the change
   in the comparison group, over the same period.
5. Check the trends before the change. If they already differed, the method does not hold.
6. Look for natural experiments, such as rollouts by country, outages and policy changes.

## Common mistakes

- Before-and-after comparisons with no comparison group.
- A comparison group that was already on a different trend.
- Reading a correlation in a dashboard as a cause.
- Ignoring that the effect may cause the supposed cause.

## Example

Researchers wanted to know whether ChatGPT reduced activity on Stack Overflow. They could not run an
experiment. So they compared Stack Overflow with its Russian and Chinese counterparts, where access
to ChatGPT is limited, and with mathematics forums, where ChatGPT was less capable. A
difference-in-differences model estimated 16% fewer weekly posts on Stack Overflow. The effect grew
over time, and it was larger for the most widely used programming languages.
