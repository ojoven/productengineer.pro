## Why it matters for a product engineer

In a collaboration product, an invitation is often a new person's first contact with it. The person
who invites wants to get work done, not to grow your user base. The person invited did not choose the
product, and may not want an account. If the path from the invitation to the shared work is long, many
invitations end at a signup form. Product engineers build both sides of that path.

## How to do it

1. Put invitations where the work needs another person: sharing a file, assigning a task, asking for
   a review.
2. Make the invitation say who sent it, what it is about and what the person can do.
3. Take the invitee straight to the shared item. Ask for an account only when it is needed.
4. Give the invitee a short first experience of their own, not the full onboarding of a new owner.
5. Measure each step: invitations sent, opened and accepted, and invitees who become active.

## Common mistakes

- Inviting people in the user's name without their clear consent.
- A generic email with no context, which looks like spam.
- A signup wall before the invitee can see what they were invited to.
- Counting invitations sent instead of invitees who become active.

## Example

Google Drive lets people share files with someone who has no Google account, when their
organization's admin turns on visitor sharing. The visitor gets an email and verifies with a code sent
to that address. They can then view, comment on or edit the file for 7 days. To continue, they verify
again from the same link. An account is not required to work on the shared file.
