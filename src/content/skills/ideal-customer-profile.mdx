## Why it matters for a product engineer

A small team cannot build for everyone. An ideal customer profile tells the engineer whose problems
come first, whose feedback weighs most and which edge cases can wait. It changes the roadmap more than
any single feature request.

## How to do it

1. Find your best customers today: the ones who use the product most, pay and stay. Superhuman found
   them by asking users how disappointed they would be without the product.
2. Write what they have in common in concrete terms: role, company size, situation, the tools they
   already use and the event that made them look for a product.
3. Write who is not a fit, and why.
4. Check the profile against data: retention, revenue per account and support load for each group.
5. Use it every week: to choose which requests go first, who you interview and which integrations
   you build.
6. Review it when your customers change. Papermark started with founders who send one deck. Today
   most new customers come for a data room for fundraising, M&A or due diligence.

## Common mistakes

- A profile so broad that it fits everyone, such as "small and medium businesses".
- Choosing the group with the most users instead of the group that gets the most value.
- Demographics without the situation that makes someone buy.
- Never writing down who is not a fit, so every request looks equally important.

## Example

Miquel Palet ranks Zernio's customers in order. First, SaaS products that embed it under their own
brand, often AI apps that want to publish what they generate. Second, agencies with their own
platform. Last, people who manage their own accounts: most of the accounts, and the least revenue. So
Zernio builds for developers and leaves the finished marketing tool to others: "we let other people
build Metricool". ([Zernio in In practice](/in-practice/zernio/))
