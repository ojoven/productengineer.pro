## Why it matters for a product engineer

The same screen can work at a desk and fail on a phone in a hurry. The setting decides many choices
that engineers make in code: how big a target is, whether sound is on, how much text people will
read, whether they can type at all. Most of these choices are made without seeing where the product
is used.

## How to do it

1. List the real settings of use: the device, the place, the time people have, and what else needs
   their hands and attention.
2. Go and see, or ask people to show you on their own device. GOV.UK suggests watching people with
   their own equipment and their usual distractions.
3. For each step of the flow, ask what the setting allows. Can they look at the screen? Hear sound?
   Use two hands? Type?
4. Test on the real device in a similar place: a phone held in one hand, outside, on a slow network.
5. Write the limits as rules the whole team can apply, such as "no step needs two hands".

## Common mistakes

- Designing on a large monitor for people who use a phone.
- Testing only in a quiet office with fast Wi-Fi.
- Asking people about their setting instead of watching it. People do not notice their own
  workarounds.
- Assuming one setting. The same product may be used at a desk, on a train and in bed.

## Example

An agent's audit of RelievApp found three techniques that fight the device they run on. Each technique
was sound on paper. The setting was a phone held in one hand, often in public or in bed. The audit
kept each technique and changed only the gesture.
