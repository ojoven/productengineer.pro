## Why it matters for a product engineer

Price is part of the product. It decides who signs up, which features people expect and how revenue
grows with use. Engineers build the billing, the limits and the meters, so the pricing model shapes
the code as much as the code shapes the price. And pricing can be tested and changed like any other
feature.

## How to do it

1. Start from value, not cost: what the product saves or earns for the customer, and what the
   [substitutes](/skills-map/discovery/market-research/substitutes/) cost.
2. Choose the value metric: the unit that grows when the customer gets more value, such as seats,
   accounts, documents or API calls.
3. Keep the plans few and easy to compare. Each plan should match a clear type of customer.
4. Make sure revenue grows when a customer grows. A flat price for a customer that becomes ten times
   bigger is money left on the table.
5. Build billing so prices and limits are configuration, not code, and you can change them.
6. Change prices for new customers first, and watch conversion and churn.

## Common mistakes

- Pricing from your costs, or from a competitor's page, without asking what it is worth.
- A value metric customers cannot predict or control.
- Too many plans and add-ons, so buyers cannot choose.
- Never raising prices, even after the product is much better.
- Pricing that punishes the behaviour you want, such as inviting colleagues.

## Example

Zernio launched with lifetime licences on AppSumo, then sold plans by the number of connected
accounts. Around February 2026 it moved to a price per account: two free with no card, then $6 a month
each up to ten, $3 up to a hundred and $1 beyond. With a single plan, Miquel Palet said, a customer
that reached 50,000 users paid no more. Papermark chose flat plans with three users included, against
DocSend's price per user. ([Zernio](/in-practice/zernio/), [Papermark](/in-practice/papermark/))
