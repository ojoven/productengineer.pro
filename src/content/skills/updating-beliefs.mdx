## Why it matters for a product engineer

Every product starts from beliefs about users, markets and what will work. Some of them will be wrong.
The question is how fast you notice, and whether you change course or defend the plan. Julia Galef
calls the useful attitude the scout mindset: wanting to see things as they are, not as you hoped.
Product engineers see real usage early, so they are often the first people able to say "we were
wrong about this".

## How to do it

1. Write down what you believe before a launch or a test, and how sure you are.
2. Say in advance what result would change your mind.
3. When the result comes, compare it with what you wrote, not with what you remember.
4. Change the belief in proportion to the evidence: a strong signal moves it more than a weak one.
5. Keep a log of changes and their reasons, so others can follow them.

## Common mistakes

- Explaining away every result that does not fit.
- Changing your mind after every anecdote, so nothing is ever settled.
- Treating a change of mind as a loss of face.
- Updating on opinions, including your own, as if they were evidence.

## Example

RelievApp, the author's own product, changed its category several times in five weeks. It launched on
14 October with a category of its own, "transitions", but nobody searches for that. On 16 November an
agent's audit found four promises at once, and the product became "a meditation app with
superpowers". Two days later it became "your personal emotional regulator". Each change had a written
reason. With no real users yet, most of them were updates of opinion, not of evidence.
