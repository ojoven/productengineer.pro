## Why it matters for a product engineer

In many products, the person who uses it, the person who pays and the person who approves are
different people with different goals. A product that users love can still fail when a buyer or an
approver says no. Engineers build the features that serve one group and cost another: an admin
control, an audit log, a permission.

## How to do it

1. List everyone who uses, buys, approves or is affected by the product, including people outside the
   company.
2. For each group, write what they want, what they fear and what they can block.
3. Find where the goals conflict, for example a user who wants speed and an approver who wants
   control.
4. Map how the business works with people who know it. EventStorming brings domain experts and
   engineers together to map the events of a business on one wall.
5. Decide whose need a feature serves first, and check that it does not block the others.

## Common mistakes

- Researching only users, and losing the sale to someone who never saw the product.
- Treating "the customer" as one person.
- Forgetting people who never touch the product but are affected by it.
- Ignoring third parties that can change the rules, such as platforms and regulators.

## Example

Zernio sells an API for posting to social networks. Its users are developers, its buyers are the
products and agencies they build for, and the people who post through it never see it. The platforms
decide what it may do. They are the group that could end the business, which sits in a grey zone they
tolerate today. ([Zernio in In practice](/in-practice/zernio/))
