## Why it matters for a product engineer

Every week of work spent on one thing is a week not spent on everything else. That cost does not
appear on any invoice, so it is easy to ignore. A small feature that "only takes two days" looks free
until you ask what else those two days could have done. When agents make code cheap, this matters
more, not less: building is no longer the limit, so choosing what not to build becomes the real
decision.

## How to do it

1. For each option, name the best alternative you give up, not just "doing nothing".
2. Compare value per week of work, not total value.
3. Include the cost of delay: what the other options lose while they wait.
4. Count time after launch too: support, maintenance and the attention a feature takes.
5. Say the "no" out loud, so the team knows what was not chosen and why.

## Common mistakes

- Judging an option only by its own value, without the alternatives.
- Small tasks that pass because each one is cheap, and together fill the month.
- Ignoring the cost of a decision that is not made.
- Comparing money now with money later as if they were the same.

## Example

Two makers in In practice answered big hypothetical offers by naming what the money would cost them.
Nathan Latka asked Papermark's Marc Seitz whether he would sell for $30 million in cash. "Dropbox
bought DocSend for 140 million, so we'll probably not sell yet." Zernio's Miquel Palet, offered a
million euros, would put it all in paid ads: not the best channel, but the most predictable. SEO
would not know what to do with it, and more people would not help the product. ([Papermark](/in-practice/papermark/), [Zernio](/in-practice/zernio/))
