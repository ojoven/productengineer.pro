## Why it matters for a product engineer

Business customers buy through a process with several people and many weeks. A funnel with defined
stages shows where deals stop and what each stage needs. Many of those needs are technical: a demo on
the customer's data, an evaluation, an integration, answers to a security review. Product engineers
who know the stages can build what removes the delays, instead of answering one request at a time.

## How to do it

1. Define each stage by what must be true to leave it, not by the activities in it.
2. Record the technical requests at each stage: demos, evaluations, integrations, security questions.
3. Measure conversion and time in each stage, for each customer segment.
4. Turn repeated requests into product: a sandbox, docs, a security page, an importer.
5. Close stalled deals by a rule, so the funnel shows the real pipeline.

## Common mistakes

- Stages named after sales activities, with no criteria to leave them.
- One funnel for small and large customers, which hides different cycle lengths.
- Treating security reviews as paperwork, not as a stage that needs engineering time.
- Keeping dead deals open, so forecasts look better than they are.

## Example

GitLab's handbook defines its sales stages: discovery, scoping, technical evaluation, proposal,
negotiation and signature. In scoping, the team may give a demo and schedules a technical evaluation
call. In technical evaluation, it confirms the technical requirements, and a proof of concept with
success criteria may take place. A new business deal that shows no activity in the first two stages is
closed automatically. The limit depends on the customer segment.
