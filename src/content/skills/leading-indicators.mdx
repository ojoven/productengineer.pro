## Why it matters for a product engineer

Revenue and retention move slowly, so you learn about a change months after you made it. A leading
indicator moves within days and predicts them. It lets a team learn from a change in the same week,
while the work is fresh. Many leading indicators are things engineers control directly: load speed,
error rate, the time it takes to finish a step.

## How to do it

1. Start from the slow outcome you care about, such as revenue, reorders or retention.
2. List what your team can move within days: error rate, load speed, time to finish a task.
3. Trace the path from those to the outcome, step by step, and add the indicators in between.
4. Check each link with data. Do users who reach the leading indicator really stay or buy more? Look
   at the correlation first, then run an experiment when you can.
5. Watch both ends. A leading indicator that rises while the outcome does not is a broken link.
6. Check the links again when the product or its users change.

## Common mistakes

- An indicator chosen because it is easy to move, not because it predicts anything.
- Assuming the link without checking it.
- Indicators too abstract for the team to act on, such as average revenue per user.
- Celebrating the leading number and forgetting the outcome.

## Example

All 21 RelievApp sessions send the same events: a rating before, a rating after, then keep it, go
again or try another. Those choices arrive within minutes of a session, so they can rank sessions long
before retention or payments can. But a rating is what someone says they feel, not proof that a
session works. ([RelievApp in In practice](/in-practice/relievapp-part-1/))
