## Why it matters for a product engineer

Many products start from an unmet need, and engineers often see one first: a workaround in the code,
a manual step nobody automated, a platform rule that blocks a feature. Naming the need precisely tells
you whether it is big enough to build a product for.

## How to do it

1. Watch what people try to do, not only what they complain about. Workarounds, copy and paste, and
   spreadsheets point to needs.
2. Ask what they do today and what it costs them in time or money.
3. Write the need without a solution in it: "apps cannot get structured data out of videos", not "an
   API for videos".
4. Check how many people have the need and how often. A need felt once a year is a weak base for a
   product.
5. Find the reason it is still unmet: a technical limit that just went away, a rule that is hard to
   pass, or a market too small for big companies.
6. Test it with something cheap before you build the full product.

## Common mistakes

- Treating a complaint as a need. Complaints are often about today's solution, not about the job.
- Writing the need as a feature.
- Ignoring users who want your product for a different reason than you expected.
- Assuming nobody solves it because you found no product. Look for workarounds too.

## Example

Mamitsu started as one feature of a coffee recipe app: it imported recipes from Instagram reels and
YouTube Shorts. The beta testers wanted that import for other things: family recipes, another coffee
app, a travel app. What they had in common was not coffee, but useful information locked inside
videos. That became the product: an API that watches a video and returns what you ask for as JSON.
