## Why it matters for a product engineer

Activation experiments test changes to the first experience: a shorter signup, a different first
task, new guidance. Every day brings new users who have not seen the old version, which makes these
tests easier to run than most. The hard part is the metric: it must predict retention, not only count
people who finished the onboarding flow.

## How to do it

1. Define activation as an action with value, within a fixed window after signup.
2. Add a quality condition when you can, so that low-value actions do not count.
3. Randomize new users only, at signup, and keep a control group with the current experience.
4. Decide the sample size and duration before the test, and do not stop early when the result looks
   good.
5. Read activation, then retention for the same users, and a quality guardrail.

## Common mistakes

- Counting completion of the onboarding flow as activation.
- Mixing existing users into a test about the first experience.
- Stopping the test on the first significant day.
- Ignoring the quality of what new users do.

## Example

Wikipedia's Growth team tested "Add a link", a task that suggests links for newcomers to add. On ten
Wikipedias, 130,179 new accounts were split at random: 20% got the usual experience, 40% got Add a
link, and 40% got an older task without suggestions. The metric was constructive activation: a first
edit within 24 hours that is not reverted within 48 hours. Add a link raised it by 16.6% over the
control. Retention rose too, and the share of reverted edits fell.
