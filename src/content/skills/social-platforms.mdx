## Why it matters for a product engineer

Social platforms let a small team reach people without a budget. Building in public, sharing what
you build and learn as you go, works especially well for engineers: real screenshots and code earn
more trust than ads. But each platform has its own audience, and the one where you are comfortable is
not always the one where your buyers are.

## How to do it

1. Choose the platform where your users spend time and talk about their work, not the one you like.
2. Post what you build and learn: a screenshot, a number, a problem you solved.
3. Use the platform's own formats: threads, short videos, carousels, posts with a link in a reply.
4. Post regularly, and reply to people. A conversation travels further than an announcement.
5. Test an idea with a post before building it. The replies are early feedback.
6. Check where your paying customers came from, and move when the audience moves.

## Common mistakes

- Only posting announcements.
- Staying on the platform that worked at launch after the buyers have moved.
- Measuring likes instead of visits, sign-ups and conversations.
- Posting the same text everywhere, ignoring each platform's norms.

## Example

Papermark started on Twitter: its co-founder bet that a tweet about the idea would get 50 likes, and
built the first version when it got 265. Three years later, its buyers are elsewhere: "None of our
customers today are on Twitter itself. Most of them are on LinkedIn."
([Papermark in In practice](/in-practice/papermark/))
