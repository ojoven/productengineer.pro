## Why it matters for a product engineer

A conversion rate looks simple: the people who reached the next step, divided by the people who
started. But who counts as "started", and how long they have to convert, can change the number more
than any feature. A product engineer who writes the definition down, and implements it exactly, makes
the rate mean the same thing every week.

## How to do it

1. Define the starting group precisely: which event, on which page, and who is excluded.
2. Define the end, including every valid end. A person who learns they are not eligible has also
   finished.
3. Set a conversion window: how long someone has to convert after they start.
4. Exclude internal users, test accounts and bots.
5. To find the step to fix, compare how many people each step loses, not only the percentages.
6. When the definition changes, mark the date, and do not compare across it.

## Common mistakes

- Different definitions in different dashboards for the same name.
- A window so short that slow but real conversions count as failures.
- Celebrating a rate that rose because fewer people entered the first step.
- Counting people who left after a test told them the service was not for them.

## Example

GOV.UK services must publish their completion rate, and the Service Manual defines it carefully. A
transaction starts when someone continues from the service's start page. "Check if you can get legal
aid" has two ends, one for people who are eligible and one for people who are not, and both count
as finished. People who leave after failing an eligibility test have not started. But someone who
applies for Carer's Allowance after the test says no has started, because they still can apply.
