## Why it matters for a product engineer

A canary release sends a new version to a small part of the traffic first and compares it with the
rest. If it is unhealthy, it is removed before most users meet it. It turns a bad release from an
outage into a small dip that only shows on a graph. For a small team, it is one of the cheapest ways
to make releases safe.

## How to do it

1. Deploy the new version to a small share of servers or users, such as 1% to 5%.
2. Compare the canary with the rest on the same metrics: errors, latency, CPU, key user actions.
   Split the graphs by version. Mixed together, a bad canary disappears into the average.
3. Wait long enough to see real traffic, including the slow paths.
4. Decide with a rule written in advance: continue, wait or roll back. Automate it where you can.
5. Widen in steps, and keep the way back open at each step.
6. Keep a separate path for real emergencies, and use it only for them.

## Common mistakes

- A canary too small or too short to see anything.
- Watching only the global average.
- A canary with no automatic way back.
- Skipping the canary for "small" changes, such as configuration or rules.

## Example

On 2 July 2019, a Cloudflare engineer deployed a minor change to a firewall rule. Rules went out
worldwide in seconds, with no staged rollout, because they exist to stop new attacks. The rule
exhausted the CPU on Cloudflare's edge and took the service down for 27 minutes. Before turning the
firewall back on, the team tested the fix in one city, on part of the traffic. Afterwards, rule
changes moved to staged rollouts, with a global push kept for active attacks.
