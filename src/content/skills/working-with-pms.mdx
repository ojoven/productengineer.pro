## Why it matters for a product engineer

A product engineer and a product manager often care about the same questions: who the user is,
what to build next, and whether it worked. That overlap is useful, but only when both people know
who decides what. Without clear roles, work gets done twice or not at all, and decisions wait for a
meeting. The engineer brings what is possible, what it costs, and what the code can do that nobody
asked for. The PM brings market, customer and business context. The best pairs think about the
problem together, instead of passing a specification from one person to the other.

## How to do it

1. Agree on who owns which decisions: scope, priorities, pricing, launch dates, technical design.
2. Join the problem early: user calls, data reviews and the first discussion of an idea.
3. Bring options with their costs, not only estimates: a simple version in two days, or the full version in three weeks.
4. Share what you see in the code and the data that the PM cannot see.
5. When you still disagree, agree who makes the final call, and then support it.

## Common mistakes

- Waiting for a finished specification before thinking about the problem.
- Treating the PM as the person who writes tickets for engineers.
- Two people deciding the same thing, so nobody owns the result.
- Saying "that is impossible" when the real answer is "that costs more than it is worth".

## Example

PostHog writes down how its product managers work with engineers. PMs do not own roadmaps or decide
what to build next. They own the systems for user discovery, the team's metrics and pricing.
Engineers choose what to work on next from shared context, and the team lead is the closest thing to
a product owner. For major calls, such as pricing or launch scope, the PM and the team lead choose one
"informed captain" who decides and sees the decision through. Many early products have no PM at all.
