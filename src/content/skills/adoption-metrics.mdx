## Why it matters for a product engineer

Adoption answers the first question about anything new: did people find it and try it? A product
engineer who ships a feature should know, in its first weeks, how many of the people who could use
it did. High adoption with low retention means the promise worked and the value did not.

## How to do it

1. Define the eligible group: the users who could use the feature, not all users.
2. Define "adopted" as a real use, such as creating a first item, not opening a page.
3. Count new adopters per week, and the share of the eligible group that has adopted so far.
4. Look at adoption together with retention: of the people who tried it, how many came back?
5. Split new users from existing users. A rise can come from either, and the two mean different
   things.
6. If adoption is low, check discovery first: did people see it at all?

## Common mistakes

- Dividing by all users instead of the users who could use the feature.
- Counting a visit as adoption.
- Celebrating a launch spike before the curve becomes flat.
- Reading page views or active users as adoption.

## Example

During the stock market crash of September 2008, Google Finance saw a surge in page views and in
seven-day active users. Those totals could not say whether new users had come for the crisis or
existing users were checking their investments. The team used adoption and retention to separate
the two groups, and to see how many new users chose to stay.
