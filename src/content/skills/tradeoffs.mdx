## Why it matters for a product engineer

Almost every real choice gives up something good to get something else: speed for safety,
flexibility for simplicity, a feature for focus. When the cost is not written down, people argue
about the benefits only, and the decision looks obvious until the cost arrives. Writing both sides
makes the choice clearer, easier to review, and easier to change when the situation changes.
Product engineers make many of these choices in code, where they are hard to see later.

## How to do it

1. List the real options, including "do nothing" and the option you do not like.
2. For each option, write what it gains and what it gives up.
3. Decide which goals matter most right now, and say why.
4. Choose, and write down what you accept as the cost.
5. Note what would make you change the decision later.

## Common mistakes

- Presenting one option as if it had no downside.
- Comparing options on different criteria.
- Forgetting the costs that arrive later: operations, maintenance, hiring.
- Never writing the decision down, so the next team repeats the debate.

## Example

Segment had split its event pipeline into more than 140 services, one per destination. A change to a
shared library meant deploying all of them, and the tests could take up to an hour. The team then
moved back to one service. Their 2018 write-up lists what they gave up: one bad destination can now crash
the service for all, in-memory caches are less effective, and a library update can break several
destinations. "The simplicity of this approach is worth the trade-off."
