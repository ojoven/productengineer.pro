## Why it matters for a product engineer

Tests never cover every device, configuration and kind of user. A staged rollout lets real use find
what tests missed, while only a few people are exposed. It turns a release from one bet into a
series of small ones, each with a chance to stop.

## How to do it

1. Start with a small share: the team, a canary group, then 1%, 5%, 25% and everyone.
2. Decide the gate for each stage before you start: the metrics, their limits, and how long to wait.
3. Compare the new group with the rest, not with yesterday. Google's SRE workbook shows why size
   matters: a version that fails 20% of requests, on a 5% canary, adds only 1% to the overall errors.
4. Stop automatically when a gate fails, and roll back.
5. Wait long enough for slow signals, such as support tickets or memory growth.
6. Apply the same rules to configuration and content, not only to code.

## Common mistakes

- Treating configuration or content updates as too small to need stages.
- Stages without gates, so the rollout continues on schedule whatever happens.
- A canary group that is not representative of real users.
- Moving faster than the metrics can show a problem.

## Example

On 19 July 2024 at 04:09 UTC, CrowdStrike sent a content update to every online Windows computer
running its sensor. A bug in its Content Validator had let bad data through. The sensor read memory out
of bounds, and Windows crashed. The update was reverted at 05:27 UTC, 78 minutes later. Microsoft estimated that 8.5 million
Windows devices were affected.
