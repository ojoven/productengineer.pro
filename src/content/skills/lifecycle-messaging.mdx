## Why it matters for a product engineer

A user's needs change from signup to habit, and sometimes to leaving. Lifecycle messages follow those
stages: a welcome, a nudge when someone stops, a tip when they are ready for more, a message to win
them back when they have gone quiet. Each message depends on an event from the product, such as
"signed up", "created a first project" or "inactive for 14 days". Product engineers build those
events, so the messages can only be as good as that data.

## How to do it

1. Map the stages of the journey, and the one action that moves a user to the next stage.
2. Send product events with stable names and properties to the messaging tool, close to real time.
3. Trigger each message from behaviour, and stop it when the user does the action it asks for.
4. Choose the channel by urgency and permission: in-app for users in the product, email for users
   away from it.
5. Keep a random group that gets no messages, and compare retention, not opens.

## Common mistakes

- A fixed schedule that sends day-3 tips to users who already did everything.
- Messages that keep asking for an action the user has already taken.
- Measuring open rates instead of the behaviour the message is for.
- Exporting lists by hand, so messages arrive late and are hard to repeat.

## Example

Spanish Wikipedia tested welcome emails for new accounts in 2022. The 22,633 new accounts were split at
random: 20% got no email, and the rest got one if they had agreed to receive it. The email did not
change how many people made a first edit. But more people edited again in the following weeks, mostly
those who signed up on mobile. The product did not trigger the emails: the team exported lists by hand
for the marketing team.
