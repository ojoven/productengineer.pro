## Why it matters for a product engineer

Acceptance criteria say what must be true when the work is done, and they are written before it
starts. They turn "I thought you meant…" into a list that everyone has read. Written as examples, they
become tests, so the definition of done is also the proof of done.

## How to do it

1. Write them before the work starts, with the people who will use, build and test it.
2. Describe behaviour a user can see, not the implementation.
3. Use concrete examples: given the state before, when the action happens, then the result you expect.
4. Cover the main case, the edge cases, and what must not change.
5. Make each one pass or fail, with no room for opinion.
6. Turn them into automated tests where you can.

## Common mistakes

- Criteria that describe the code, not the behaviour.
- Vague words: "fast", "easy", "intuitive".
- Criteria written after the work, to match what was built.
- Only the main case.

## Example

An agent's audit of RelievApp wrote a check for every new or edited scene: one gesture, no way to
fail, the control on the subject, at most two texts and none over the subject, every input answered,
a true time label, and readable at 390 px in both looks. A reviewer, or a test, can check each item.
([RelievApp in In practice](/in-practice/relievapp-part-1/))
