## Why it matters for a product engineer

Users should be able to understand what a product does for them and to change it: turn a setting off,
leave a plan, delete their data. Every product team is tempted to make some of these paths harder,
because a confused user does not cancel. That trades long-term trust for short-term numbers, and it
increasingly breaks consumer law. Product engineers build these flows, so they are often the first to
see when a design works against the user.

## How to do it

1. Make leaving as easy as joining: the same number of steps, in the same channel.
2. Use honest defaults; ask before you opt people into anything that costs money or shares data.
3. Use clear labels on every choice, with no confusing double negatives or guilt-based wording.
4. Test the exit paths with real users, and measure how long they take.
5. When you see a design that works against users, say so, with the data.

## Common mistakes

- A sign-up that takes one click and a cancellation that takes many pages.
- Pre-ticked boxes and settings that quietly reset after an update.
- Measuring success by fewer cancellations instead of fewer reasons to cancel.
- Treating a cancellation flow as a place to win the user back at any cost.

## Example

In September 2025, Amazon agreed to pay $2.5 billion to settle a case brought by the US Federal Trade
Commission. The FTC said Amazon had tricked customers into joining Prime and then made it hard
to cancel. Internally, Amazon called the cancellation process "Iliad", after the Greek poem about a long
siege. According to the FTC's complaint, leadership slowed or rejected changes that would have made
cancelling easier. Amazon admitted no wrongdoing. The settlement says that cancelling "must not be
difficult, costly, confusing or time consuming".
