## Why it matters for a product engineer

A mission says why the product exists and whom it serves, in one sentence. It changes rarely, so it
is the tiebreaker when two good options pull in different directions. For an engineer, a clear mission
answers small questions that nobody has time to decide: which feature to cut, which user to serve
first, which shortcut is wrong.

## How to do it

1. Write why the product exists and whom it serves, in one sentence people can remember.
2. Leave out features, technologies and numbers. They change; Marty Cagan expects a mission to last a
   decade or more.
3. Test it against real decisions. Would it have told you what to do in your last three hard choices?
4. Keep it apart from the product vision, the strategy and this year's objectives, and show how they
   connect.
5. Repeat it where decisions happen: planning, reviews, onboarding.
6. Help each team see how its own work contributes to it.

## Common mistakes

- A mission so general it fits any company, such as "delight our customers".
- A mission that names a product or a technology.
- Changing it every time the plan changes.
- A mission on the wall that no decision ever uses.

## Example

RelievApp made five promises in five weeks, from "transitions" to "your personal emotional
regulator". The reason it exists did not move. Its maker finds meditation helpful but hard to stay
with, and wanted a way in with a picture and something to do with the hands. The promise is strategy
and can change; the reason is closer to a mission.
