## Why it matters for a product engineer

A vision tells you which shortcuts are safe. If you know where the product should be in a few years,
you can tell a quick fix that is fine from one that blocks the road. A good vision also rules things
out. If it rules nothing out, it does not help anyone decide.

## How to do it

1. Describe the future for the user, three to five years out: what they can do then that they cannot
   do now.
2. Add what the company must look like to get there: size, funding, channels.
3. Write what you will not do. This is the part that helps decisions.
4. Make it short enough that everyone on the team can repeat it.
5. Connect it to the [roadmap](/skills-map/strategy/product-planning/roadmaps/): each big item should
   move toward it.
6. Review it once a year, or when the market changes a lot. A vision can change. It should not change
   every month.

## Common mistakes

- A vision so general that it fits any company.
- Confusing the vision with a list of features.
- A vision only the founders know.
- Changing direction every quarter, so nobody trusts it.
- No "no": a direction that never stops anything.

## Example

Miquel Palet left Ucademy to test whether he could build a company without venture capital, less
dependent on paid ads, and remote. That direction rules things out at Zernio: no plan to sell, no
stock options, all profit back into growth, and no marketing tool of its own that would compete with
customers. Papermark's next goal is $10M ARR, still bootstrapped.
