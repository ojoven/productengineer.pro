## Why it matters for a product engineer

The number of users a test needs depends on the starting rate and on the smallest effect worth
finding. Working it out before the test tells you how long to run it, or whether to run it at all. A
test that is too small produces noise that looks like a result, and teams act on it.

## How to do it

1. Write the baseline: the current rate of the metric, such as 5% of visitors who sign up.
2. Decide the smallest effect worth detecting. Smaller effects need many more users.
3. Calculate the sample. Evan Miller's rule of thumb is n = 16 × p × (1 − p) ÷ δ² per group, where δ
   is the absolute difference you want to detect.
4. Divide by your traffic to get the duration. If it takes many months, test a bigger change or use
   another method.
5. Fix the sample size in advance, and do not stop early because the result looks good.
6. With little traffic, prefer fewer variants, bigger changes, or qualitative methods.

## Common mistakes

- Starting a test without knowing how long it must run.
- Stopping at the first significant result. Testing after every visitor can make a "5%" false
  positive rate about 26%.
- Splitting small traffic between many variants.
- Hoping to detect a 2% lift with a few hundred visitors.

## Example

An audit of RelievApp found that its ad budget, about €50 a month, buys a few hundred visits at best.
Split between four front doors, that is too few visitors per door to compare anything beyond the first
tap. The audit's conclusion: the choice between the four had to be made by judgement or by cheap
organic tests, not by the budget. ([RelievApp in In practice](/in-practice/relievapp-part-1/))
