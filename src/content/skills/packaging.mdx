## Why it matters for a product engineer

Packaging decides what goes into each plan and where a customer has to upgrade. It moves conversion
and expansion as much as the price does. It also creates engineering work: entitlements, feature
gates, usage limits and the messages people see when they reach one.

## How to do it

1. Group your customers by the value they get and their budget. Each plan should match one group.
2. Put the features every customer needs in every plan. Put the features only bigger customers need,
   such as roles, audit logs or single sign-on, in higher plans.
3. Place the upgrade points where value grows: more users, more usage, a feature a team needs once it
   gets serious.
4. Keep the number of plans small enough to compare at a glance.
5. Build entitlements as data, so you can move a feature between plans without a release.
6. Watch which plan people choose and where they hit limits, and adjust.

## Common mistakes

- Plans that differ only in numbers nobody understands.
- A free plan that covers what most paying customers need.
- Gating the feature that shows the product's value, so people never see it.
- Limits that surprise the customer in the middle of their work.
- Being afraid of an expensive plan. Some customers want it.

## Example

Papermark has seven plans, from free to €999 a month billed yearly. Four of them are data rooms,
because most new customers now come for deals. Iuliia Shnai said they hesitated to add the highest
tier, and it became the fastest-growing one. The amounts are also in the public code, in
`ee/stripe/utils.ts`. ([Papermark in In practice](/in-practice/papermark/))
