## Why it matters for a product engineer

A funnel shows where a flow loses people, so engineering time goes to the step that matters. It is
often the first chart to build after shipping a flow. It turns "onboarding feels slow" into a number
for each step.

## How to do it

1. Define the last step first: the action that means success, such as the
   [activation event](/skills-map/activation/time-to-value/activation-events/).
2. Choose three to five steps that every user must pass. With more steps, the real problem is harder
   to see.
3. Set a conversion window that fits the flow: one session for a checkout, 7 to 14 days for
   onboarding.
4. Wait until a few hundred users have entered the flow before you read it.
5. Look for the largest loss in number of users, not only the lowest percentage.
6. Split that step by device, channel or plan. Then watch
   [session replays](/skills-map/measurement/qualitative-evaluation/session-replays/) or talk to
   people who left there.

## Common mistakes

- Measuring steps as a strict sequence when users can do them in any order.
- Renaming an event in the middle of the period, so a step seems to collapse.
- Fixing the step with the lowest rate when another step loses more people.
- Adding more persuasion at the step where people leave, instead of removing what stops them.

## Example

Buffer sent visitors from its home page to the pricing page before signup, and 57% of them left
between those two pages. In a test, Buffer sent people straight to signup, and signup conversion went
up 17%. Simon Heaton, its Director of Marketing, said removing friction did better than adding
persuasion in their tests: "subtract before you add".
