## Why it matters for a product engineer

Not every interested company is a good customer. Qualification checks fit, need and the ability to buy
before the team spends weeks on a deal. In a product with self-serve signup, usage data can do part of
this work. A team that invites colleagues and uses the core feature every day is a better lead than a
single signup. Product engineers can put those signals into the data the sales team sees.

## How to do it

1. Write down the ideal customer profile: company size, industry, the problem, the buyer.
2. Choose a framework that fits the deal size, and make its questions part of the sales process.
3. Define product signals that show intent, such as several active users from one company, or use of
   a paid feature during a trial.
4. Send those signals to the sales tools automatically, by account rather than by person.
5. Compare qualified leads with closed deals every quarter, and adjust the rules.

## Common mistakes

- Treating every signup as a sales lead.
- Scoring leads on page views and email opens alone.
- Rules that nobody reviews after the first quarter.
- Sending personal data to sales tools without a clear legal basis.

## Example

Two frameworks are common. IBM designed BANT in the 1950s: budget, authority, need and timeline. In the
1990s, Jack Napoli and Dick Dunkel created MEDDIC at the software company PTC. It asks about metrics,
the economic buyer, the decision criteria, the decision process, the pain and an internal champion.
Qwilr suggests BANT for small and medium businesses, and MEDDIC for enterprise sales and larger deals.
