## Why it matters for a product engineer

A migration asks users to do work for your reasons: learn a new screen, move their data, change a
plan. Each step where they must act is a step where some of them stop. The engineering decides most
of that cost. If accounts, data and settings move automatically, users only need to learn the new
product. If they must export and import by hand, many will not, or will leave. Product engineers build
the tooling that makes the move cheap.

## How to do it

1. Run old and new side by side for a period, so users can move at their own pace.
2. Move data and settings automatically, and keep the same sign-in if you can.
3. Let the two versions work together during the transition, so nobody is cut off.
4. Offer an export for people who do not want to move.
5. Track how many users moved, and contact the rest before each deadline.

## Common mistakes

- A forced move with no clear benefit for the user.
- Data that is lost or changed on the way, found only after the old system is gone.
- One final date with no transition period.
- Ignoring the edge cases, such as shared accounts or paid features, until support tickets arrive.

## Example

On 28 February 2025, Microsoft announced that Skype would close on 5 May 2025, and offered Teams Free
in its place. Skype users could sign in to Teams with their Skype account. Their chats and contacts
appeared there automatically. Until May, Skype and Teams users could call and chat with each other.
People who did not want Teams could export their chats, contacts and call history instead. Existing
paid subscriptions continued until the end of their next renewal period.
