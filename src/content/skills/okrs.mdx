## Why it matters for a product engineer

OKRs connect the team's work to a result someone cares about. When the key results measure outcomes,
engineers can choose how to reach them, and propose a better way than the one first planned. When
they measure tasks, the team ships a list and nobody knows whether it helped.

## How to do it

1. Write one objective: qualitative, ambitious, and clear about what should change.
2. Add two to four key results that show the objective was reached. Each has a number, a starting
   point and a date, such as "from 11% to 5% by June".
3. Check every key result: is it an outcome or a task? "Launch the new onboarding" is a task.
   "Activation from 30% to 40%" is an outcome.
4. Say whether the OKR is committed (it must be met) or aspirational (reaching 70% is good).
5. Let the team choose the projects. Review progress every week or two, in a few minutes.
6. Grade it at the end of the cycle, and write what you learned before you set the next one.

## Common mistakes

- A task list with numbers attached.
- Too many OKRs, so nothing is a priority.
- Key results nobody can measure until the quarter is over.
- Paying people for hitting them, which turns ambitious goals into safe ones.
- Setting them and only looking again at the end.

## Example

Allbirds, the shoe company, wanted its shoes to be carbon neutral. Its OKR, as What Matters shows it,
mixes the two kinds of key result: shares of material that the product must reach, and an action the
company can simply decide to take. Reading each line and asking "outcome or task?" is the main habit
OKRs need.
