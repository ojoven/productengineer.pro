## Why it matters for a product engineer

Lifetime value is how much a customer brings in over the whole time they stay. It sets the limit on
what you can spend to get a customer, and it shows which customers are worth building for. Because it
depends on how long people stay, the product work that improves retention often raises it more than a
price change does.

## How to do it

1. Measure average revenue per account per month.
2. Multiply by the gross margin, so you count what you keep, not what you invoice.
3. Measure churn: the share of customers who leave each month.
4. Divide: margin per month divided by monthly churn gives a simple lifetime value. With 5% churn,
   an average customer stays about 20 months.
5. Calculate it for each segment, plan and channel. The average hides the differences.
6. Compare it with what a customer costs to acquire, and with how long that cost takes to pay back.

## Common mistakes

- Using revenue instead of margin.
- One number for all customers.
- Assuming young customers will stay as long as old ones, before you have the data.
- A lifetime value based on a few months of history, presented as fact.
- Raising prices to grow lifetime value while churn quietly rises.

## Example

Zernio's average account pays about €50 a month, and Miquel Palet gave a lifetime value of about €700,
or 14 months of revenue. The average hides a big gap. Integrators, who pay the most, churn about 2%,
while end users churn 17%. At those rates an integrator stays about four years and an end user about
six months. ([Zernio in In practice](/in-practice/zernio/))
