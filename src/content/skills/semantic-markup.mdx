## Why it matters for a product engineer

Semantic HTML uses the element that means what you want: a `button`, a heading, a `label`, a `nav`.
Assistive technology reads that structure, search engines read it, and so does the next engineer.
The right element does most of the accessibility work for free. ARIA exists for the gaps, not to
replace the right element.

## How to do it

1. Use headings in order, `h1` to `h3`, to show the structure of the page, not to make text large.
2. Use landmarks: `header`, `nav`, `main` and `footer`, so people can jump between regions.
3. Give every form field a `label` connected to it.
4. Use `button` for actions and `a href` for navigation, never a `div` for either.
5. Use lists for lists and tables for tabular data.
6. Add ARIA only when no HTML element does the job, and test the result with a screen reader.

## Common mistakes

- `div` and `span` for everything, with ARIA roles added on top.
- Headings chosen for their size, so the outline makes no sense.
- Placeholder text instead of a label.
- `aria-hidden="true"` on content that people need.

## Example

WebAIM's 2026 scan of the top million home pages found 133 ARIA attributes per page on average, more than
six times as many as in 2019. Pages with ARIA had more accessibility errors than pages without it. WebAIM is
careful: those pages were also more complex, so ARIA is not proven to cause the errors. But more ARIA
did not mean more accessible.
