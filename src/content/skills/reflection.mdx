## Why it matters for a product engineer

Experience only teaches you something if you look back at it. Without reflection, ten years of work
can be one year repeated ten times. Product engineers make many bets: features, designs, estimates,
technical choices. Most of them are never checked against what actually happened. Writing down what
you expected, and comparing it with the result a few months later, is one of the cheapest ways to
improve your judgement. Retrospectives and postmortems do the same for a team.

## How to do it

1. Before a project, write down what you expect: the result, the effort, the main risk.
2. After it ships, wait long enough to see the real effect, then write down what happened.
3. Compare the two, and ask why they differ: missing information, bad luck or a wrong belief.
4. Keep a running document of your work, and review it every few weeks or months.
5. Choose one or two changes to try next time, and check later whether they helped.

## Common mistakes

- Reflecting only after failures, so successes teach nothing.
- Judging a decision only by its outcome, not by what was known at the time.
- Retrospectives that produce long lists and no changes.
- Writing down what you did but never what it achieved.

## Example

Julia Evans, a software engineer, writes about keeping a "brag document": a running list of the work
she has done. It helps with promotions, but she finds it more useful for reflection. She suggests going
back a few months after a launch to write down what actually happened. Then she asks questions such as:
which projects had the effect I wanted, and which did not? Why? Some people update the document every
two weeks; she prefers one long review every six months or every year.
