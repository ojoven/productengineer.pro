## Why it matters for a product engineer

Instructions explain how to do something at the moment it is needed. The best interface needs few of
them, but complex tasks always need some: a setup step, an import, an API key. Instructions that
people skip, or that need their own explanation, show where the design needs work. That is a signal
for the engineer, not only for the writer.

## How to do it

1. First try to remove the need: a better default, a clearer control, one less step.
2. Put the instruction next to the control it is about, at the moment it is needed.
3. Write it as numbered steps, one action per step, in the order people do them.
4. Start each step with the verb and name the control as it appears on the screen.
5. Use verbs that work with any input: mouse, keyboard, touch or voice.
6. Watch people follow it. Where they stop, change the interface or the step.

## Common mistakes

- A long text before the task, which people skip.
- Verbs for one input only, such as "click" or "swipe".
- Directions by colour or position: "the button on the right".
- Instructions that no longer match the interface after a release.

## Example

An agent's audit of RelievApp found that its visual language had to be studied before the first
session. A guide taught it in order: you, your thoughts, your edge, colour, a meaning for each scene,
then a legend. On "This is your edge", the guide pointed at a line that was hard to see in the minimal
look. The audit's point: the body needs none of this. A scene where you tap where it hurts needs no
lesson. ([RelievApp in In practice](/in-practice/relievapp-part-1/))
