## Why it matters for a product engineer

An MVP is where engineering and product meet. The engineer often decides how small the first version
can be while it still does its one job well. Build too much and you learn late. Build something
broken and you learn nothing, because people leave for the wrong reason.

## How to do it

1. Write down the one assumption that would end the idea if it were wrong. "Founders want to share a
   deck as a link they control" is an assumption. "We need folders" is not.
2. Choose the cheapest test that gives a real answer. Sometimes that is a post, a landing page or a
   manual service, not software (see
   [Concierge and Wizard of Oz tests](/skills-map/discovery/opportunity-validation/concierge-experiments/)).
3. Set a time limit before you start, such as a weekend or two weeks. Then cut scope until the work
   fits the limit.
4. Write down the result that would prove the assumption wrong, before anyone sees the product.
5. Put it in front of people who have the problem today, and watch what they do, not what they say.

## Common mistakes

- Calling the first release of the full roadmap an MVP.
- Building one part of the final product, like one wheel of a car, that nobody can use on its own.
- Testing with friends who are not the target users.
- Skipping tracking, then arguing about what happened.
- Keeping the MVP code as the foundation without deciding to.

## Example

Before Marc Seitz wrote any code for Papermark, he bet that a tweet about the idea would get 50
likes. It got 265. The first version was a weekend build on Next.js and Vercel: upload a PDF, share a
link, see who read which page. The first customers asked to pay before the payment flow was finished.
([Papermark in In practice](/in-practice/papermark/))
