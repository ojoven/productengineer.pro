## Why it matters for a product engineer

A survey tells you how common something is, once interviews have told you what to ask. An engineer can
add a short survey to the product in an afternoon, and it is often the cheapest way to put a number on
a question. Written badly, it gives confident answers that are wrong.

## How to do it

1. Write the decision the survey should inform. Then keep only the questions that inform it.
2. Ask about the past and the present, not the future. People cannot predict their own behaviour.
3. Prefer closed questions. Make the options cover every case without overlapping, and add "Not sure"
   or "Does not apply".
4. One idea per question: if a question has "and" in it, split it.
5. Use neutral words and balanced scales, with as many positive options as negative ones.
6. Ask the right people at the right moment, for example in the product, just after the action you
   ask about. Try the survey on two or three people before you send it.

## Common mistakes

- Asking what people would do or would pay.
- Leading questions, such as a scale that follows "We are committed to five stars".
- Counting only the people who chose to answer, and calling it everyone. See
  [sampling bias](/skills-map/measurement/statistical-reasoning/sampling-bias/).
- So many questions that people stop or answer at random.
- Percentages from a dozen answers.

## Example

Superhuman emailed its users a survey with four questions. The key one was "How would you feel if you
could no longer use Superhuman?", answered very, somewhat or not disappointed. In summer 2017 only
22% said very disappointed, below the 40% that Sean Ellis saw in startups with strong traction. The
team focused on the users who would miss it most, and three quarters of product work later the score
was 58%.
