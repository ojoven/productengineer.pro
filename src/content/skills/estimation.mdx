## Why it matters for a product engineer

People need to know roughly when work will be done, to plan launches, sales and other work. An
estimate is a forecast, not a promise. A product engineer who gives honest ranges, and who can
change the scope to fit the time, is more useful than one who gives a precise date that turns out
wrong.

## How to do it

1. Give a range and a confidence level, such as "two to four weeks, 85% likely", not one date.
2. Break the work down until each piece is understood. The pieces nobody understands are where the
   time goes.
3. Compare with similar past work, not with how the work feels today.
4. Forecast from the team's real throughput. A Monte Carlo simulation on past delivery gives a range
   with probabilities.
5. For a new idea, decide first how much time the problem is worth, then shape a solution that
   fits.
6. Update the forecast when you learn something, and say so early.

## Common mistakes

- One number, heard as a promise.
- Estimating only the coding, not the review, testing and release.
- Adding pressure to the estimate instead of removing scope.
- Never checking the old estimates against what happened.

## Example

A Basecamp customer asked for a calendar. A full calendar could take six months, so the team asked
what six weeks could do. They called the customer and asked when she wanted a calendar. Her office
kept bookings for its meeting rooms on a calendar drawn on a wall, and she drove there to check it.
The real need was to see free spaces from home, not everything a calendar does.
