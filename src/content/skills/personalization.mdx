## Why it matters for a product engineer

Personalization shows each user what fits them: their language, their past choices, the items they
are likely to want. Done well, it saves people time. Done badly, it shows confident guesses, and it is
hard to tell whether it helps at all. A product engineer builds the data, the ranking and the test, so
they can keep each step measurable and simple enough to explain.

## How to do it

1. Start with what users tell you or do in plain sight: language, location, recent items, saved
   choices.
2. Write simple rules first, such as "show recently viewed items". They set the bar that a model must
   beat.
3. Choose the business outcome before you build, such as bookings or finished tasks. Clicks are only a
   proxy, and they can mislead.
4. Run a randomized test against what users see today, and focus the analysis on users for whom the
   new version changes what they see.
5. Measure the time the model adds to each page. A slower page can cost more than better ranking
   gains.

## Common mistakes

- Judging a model by its offline score alone.
- Optimizing clicks that do not turn into the outcome you want.
- Hiding why something was recommended, so users cannot correct it.
- Building a model where a sorted list would do.

## Example

In 2019, Booking.com had about 150 machine-learned models in production, many of them to adapt what
travellers see. Its data scientists reported that a model with better offline performance often did
not bring more business value. One reason was optimizing a proxy, such as clicks, that did not turn
into bookings. So every model is judged in randomized trials on business metrics. In a separate test,
about 30% more latency cost about 0.5% in conversion.
