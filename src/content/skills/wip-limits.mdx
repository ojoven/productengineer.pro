## Why it matters for a product engineer

Every item a team starts and does not finish waits somewhere: in review, in testing, in someone's
head. Too much work in progress makes each item take longer and hides where it is stuck. A limit
makes the team finish before it starts, so value reaches users sooner and blockers become visible.

## How to do it

1. Make all the work visible on one board, including the invisible kind: support, fixes, reviews
   and meetings that take real time.
2. Set a limit for each column. For example, with four pairs of developers, no more than four cards
   in "In development".
3. When a column is full, help move a card forward before you pull a new one.
4. Measure cycle time, from start to done, and throughput, the items finished per week.
5. Watch where cards pile up. That column is the bottleneck, and the place to improve.
6. Lower the limits slowly, until problems appear, then fix the problems.

## Common mistakes

- Limits on paper that nobody respects when a request is urgent.
- Not counting invisible work, so the board looks fine while people are overloaded.
- Limits so high that they never stop anything.
- Measuring how busy people are instead of how fast work finishes.

## Example

Siemens Health Services had about 50 teams running 30-day sprints. The last week of each sprint was
"a mad rush" to claim points, with hurried testing. Teams started too many features, and most were
not finished until the end of the release. The program moved every team to Kanban at once, with
WIP limits. The cumulative flow diagram then showed higher throughput in development than in
testing across all teams, so management invested heavily in test automation.
