## Why it matters for a product engineer

Every technical decision trades something for something else: speed for safety, simplicity for
flexibility, cost now for cost later. A product engineer weighs these against the product's stage and
goals, not only against technical taste. Writing the trade-off down makes it possible to review it
later, when the situation changes.

## How to do it

1. Name what you gain and what you give up, in concrete terms. "Faster" is not enough: faster for whom,
   and at what cost?
2. Tie each trade-off to the product's stage. An early product needs speed of learning more than scale.
3. Ask how reversible the decision is. Easy-to-reverse decisions need less analysis.
4. Write a short decision record: the context, the decision, the status and the consequences, good and
   bad.
5. Say what would make you change your mind, such as a load level or a team size.
6. Review old records when that condition happens.

## Common mistakes

- Presenting a decision as free, with no cost.
- Choosing the architecture of a large company for a small product.
- Decisions that live only in a chat thread or one person's memory.
- Never revisiting a trade-off after the context changed.

## Example

Segment sends customer events to over 100 kinds of destinations. It had grown to more than 140
services, one per destination, and three full-time engineers spent most of their time keeping them
alive. The team merged them into one service. In their post they listed what they gave up and why
they accepted each loss. The team made 32 improvements to its shared libraries under the old design,
and 46 in the year after the merge.
