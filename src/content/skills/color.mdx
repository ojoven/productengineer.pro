## Why it matters for a product engineer

Colour in an interface should mean something: a state, an action, a category, a warning. A small
palette with defined roles keeps the product consistent and easy to read. Contrast has to work for
everyone, in light and dark mode. Engineers turn the palette into tokens, so they decide whether the
rules hold everywhere.

## How to do it

1. Define a small palette, and give each colour a role: text, background, action, success, warning,
   error.
2. Store the colours as tokens with names that describe the role, such as `danger`, not `red-500`.
3. Check contrast for every pair of text and background, including on images and in dark mode. Normal
   text needs a ratio of at least 4.5 to 1.
4. Never use colour as the only signal. Add an icon, a label or a pattern.
5. Build dark mode from the same roles, with colours checked again, not inverted.
6. Add a check to the build that fails on a raw colour value outside the tokens.

## Common mistakes

- Grey text that looks elegant and is hard to read.
- Red and green as the only difference between two states.
- Many near-identical colours that nobody chose on purpose.
- Light text on a light, animated background.
- Dark mode made by inverting colours.

## Example

RelievApp uses ten colours and no others, each with a meaning: coral is the hard end, sun is energy
that works for you, mint is settled. A session moves from its hard colour to its settled one, so the
colour itself shows the change. An earlier audit had found the opposite problem: white text over a
pale mint sphere that almost disappeared. ([RelievApp in In practice](/in-practice/relievapp-part-1/))
