## Why it matters for a product engineer

Engagement metrics show how often and how deeply people use what you build. They are also the easiest
numbers to inflate. A notification or a dark pattern can raise daily active users while the product
gets worse. An engineer who knows which actions show real value can track those, and can tell a team
when a rise in engagement means nothing.

## How to do it

1. Start from the goal, then the signal, then the metric, as in Google's HEART method. "People find
   documents faster" comes before "searches per user".
2. Count actions that deliver value, such as a document read to the end, not opens or page loads.
3. Measure frequency and depth separately: how many days a month someone is active, and how much
   they do on those days.
4. Look at the distribution, not only the average. The power user curve, a histogram of active days
   per month, shows whether there is a group that uses the product every day.
5. Compare engagement with the product's natural rhythm. A tax tool used once a year can be healthy.
6. Watch engagement next to retention and revenue, so that a rise caused by noise is easy to see.

## Common mistakes

- Treating time in the product as good in itself. Sometimes the best result is a short visit.
- Counting notification opens as engagement.
- One ratio, such as DAU/MAU, with no look at the users behind it.
- Engagement goals with no link to value, which reward the wrong features.

## Example

Papermark sells engagement metrics. A founder who shares a deck sees the number of visits, how long
each visitor stayed, how much of the document they saw, and the time spent on each page, split by
version. All of it comes from one event, sent for every page viewed. ([Papermark in In practice](/in-practice/papermark/))
