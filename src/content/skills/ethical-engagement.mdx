## Why it matters for a product engineer

Deceptive patterns, also called dark patterns, trick people into actions they did not intend: a
hidden way to cancel, an extra item added to the basket, a decline link that shames the user. They
raise short-term numbers, damage trust, and many are now illegal. Engineers build them, often from a
ticket that only asks for more conversions. That also puts engineers in a good position to say no.

## How to do it

1. Apply one test to every flow: would users approve if they saw how it works?
2. Make leaving as easy as joining: cancel with the same method people used to sign up.
3. Show the full terms at the moment of the choice: the price, when you charge, renewal and how to
   cancel.
4. Give the "no" option the same visual weight as the "yes".
5. Put guardrail metrics next to conversion: refunds, complaints and cancellations soon after sign-up.
6. Walk through each flow as your most vulnerable user, such as someone in a hurry or with low
   digital skills.

## Common mistakes

- Confirmshaming, such as "No thanks, I don't like saving money".
- Asking again and again after people have said no.
- A/B tests that choose the variant that confuses people most, because it converts best.
- Copying a pattern because a large company uses it.

## Example

In 2023 the US Federal Trade Commission sued Amazon over Prime. The FTC said that buying without Prime
was harder to find, and that people who tried to cancel went through several pages of offers first.
In its words, the cancellation process was built "not to enable subscribers to cancel, but to stop
them." In September 2025, Amazon agreed to pay $2.5 billion.
