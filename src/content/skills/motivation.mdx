## Why it matters for a product engineer

In BJ Fogg's model, a behaviour happens when motivation, ability and a prompt meet at the same moment.
A product rarely creates motivation. It can make the action easy when motivation is there, prompt at
the right moment, and avoid replacing the reason people already have with a weaker one. Points,
payments and penalties are often built by engineers, and each one changes why people act.

## How to do it

1. Find the moments when motivation is already high, such as the moment the problem appears or just
   after a success. Put the prompt there.
2. When an action does not happen, check the three parts in order: is there a prompt, is the action
   easy enough, and only then, is motivation enough?
3. Raise ability before you try to raise motivation: fewer steps, less time, less thinking.
4. Connect the product to what people already want, such as progress, belonging or relief, not to
   your own metrics.
5. Be careful with rewards and penalties. They can change the reason people act, and the old reason
   may not come back when you remove them.
6. Measure what people do after an incentive ends, not only while it runs.

## Common mistakes

- Treating low use as a motivation problem and adding persuasive copy, when the action is hard.
- Points or badges for something people already did for their own reasons.
- Prompts at moments when motivation is low, such as a notification at a random time.
- Paying people for referrals or reviews without checking what happens to quality.

## Example

Uri Gneezy and Aldo Rustichini studied ten private day-care centres in Haifa. In six of them, they
added a small fine for parents who came more than ten minutes late. Late pick-ups almost doubled. When
the fine was removed twelve weeks later, they stayed high. The title of their paper gives their
reading: a fine is a price.
