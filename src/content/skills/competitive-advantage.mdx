## Why it matters for a product engineer

A difference that anyone can copy in a month does not last. A competitive advantage is the part that
stays hard to copy: data, network effects, approvals, relationships, deep focus. Knowing which one you
have tells engineers where to invest for years, and which parts can be bought, borrowed or generated.

## How to do it

1. List what a well-funded rival would need to match you, and how long each part would take.
2. Separate what is easy to copy (features, screens, endpoints) from what is slow to copy (data,
   users, approvals, trust, a brand people search for).
3. Check that the slow part is real today, not a plan. Network effects with a hundred users are not
   yet an advantage.
4. Invest in what makes it stronger with use: more data, more connections, better integrations.
5. Name the threats: a platform that ships your product as a feature, a customer that builds it in
   house, a rule that changes.
6. Review it every year. Advantages wear out.

## Common mistakes

- Calling the code an advantage. Code is easier to copy than ever.
- Confusing being first with being hard to copy.
- Ignoring platform risk: the company whose platform you build on can become your competitor.
- Thinking an advantage in one market carries over to the next one.

## Example

Miquel Palet says mapping endpoints is easy, so Zernio's advantage is not its code. It is the approved
apps, the raised quotas and the relations with each platform, which take months to get. Papermark's
advantage is its open core: nobody can charge less than free, and a bank can audit the code. Mamitsu's
maker names the risk for products built on the AI labs' models: a lab can ship them as a feature.
