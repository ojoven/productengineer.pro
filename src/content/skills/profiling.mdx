## Why it matters for a product engineer

Profiling replaces guesses with measurements. The slow part of a system is rarely where the team
expects it to be. A profile shows a product engineer where time and memory really go, so a fix
targets the cause, and its effect can be checked afterwards.

## How to do it

1. Start from a symptom people feel and a number: a slow page, a slow endpoint, a p95 that grew.
2. Reproduce it with realistic data, or profile production with a sampling profiler. Sampling costs
   little, so it changes the program's behaviour less than tracing every call.
3. Choose the profile that fits the symptom: CPU for busy code, a query plan for a slow query, the
   browser's Performance panel for the interface, a memory profile for growth over time.
4. Look for the widest part of the profile, not the most surprising one. Fix one thing at a time.
5. Measure again under the same conditions. Keep the numbers from before and after with the change.

## Common mistakes

- Optimizing before profiling.
- Profiling on a laptop with a tiny database, and trusting the result.
- Tracing every function call, which slows the program and changes what you measure.
- Reading the x-axis of a flame graph as time.
- Stopping after one fix without measuring again.

## Example

Brendan Gregg invented flame graphs while working on a MySQL performance problem. The usual profilers
gave him "walls of text". Tracing every function call cost too much and disturbed the program, so he
switched to timed sampling. Samples lose the order of calls, so he gave up time on the x-axis and
sorted the stacks to merge identical frames. The picture became readable, and he released flame
graphs in December 2011.
