## Why it matters for a product engineer

Every change carries a belief about why it will help. When you write that belief down before the
test, a feature becomes an experiment, and it can teach you something even when it fails. It also
stops the team from inventing a story after the results arrive. A product engineer who writes the
hypothesis can also see which parts of it are cheap to test first.

## How to do it

1. Write it in one sentence: the change, the metric you expect to move, and why. Barry O'Reilly's
   template works: "We believe [this change] will result in [this outcome]. We will know when [we
   see this signal]."
2. List the premises under the reason. Each premise is a smaller hypothesis.
3. Test the riskiest premise with the cheapest experiment, before you build the full feature.
4. Set the threshold in advance: which result means keep, and which means stop.
5. After the test, write down which belief was wrong, not only which version won.

## Common mistakes

- A hypothesis with no number, such as "users will like it".
- Writing the hypothesis after looking at the data.
- Testing many changes at once, so a failure explains nothing.
- Treating a failed test as wasted work instead of a corrected belief.

## Example

Etsy built infinite scroll for its search results, because "more items, faster" seemed clearly
better. In the A/B test, people with infinite scroll saw fewer items, clicked fewer, and bought fewer
items from search. Only then did the team test the two premises on their own. More results per page
barely helped and did not change purchases. Search results that were slowed down on purpose changed
nothing. Etsy removed infinite scroll.
