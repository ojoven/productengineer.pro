## Why it matters for a product engineer

Users experience more than your screens. A journey map lays out what they do, think and feel from the
first time they hear about the product to long after they use it, including the steps outside it. It
shows where the real friction sits, and which moments nobody on the team owns.

## How to do it

1. Choose one user and one goal. A map for everyone at once is too vague to use.
2. List the moments, from first contact to the end of the goal, including what happens offline or in
   other tools. Base them on interviews and data, not on the team's memory.
3. Group similar moments and keep the most important ones. A map with too many steps is not read.
4. For each moment, write what the user does, thinks and feels, and what the product does today.
5. Mark the gaps: moments where the user needs something and the product does nothing.
6. Put the team's ideas and owners under the moments they serve. Moments with no ideas and no owner
   are opportunities.

## Common mistakes

- Mapping only the screens you already have.
- A map built from assumptions, never checked with users.
- A beautiful poster that changes no decision.
- Too much detail, so nobody sees the important moments.

## Example

In 2011, Airbnb drew its customer journey as a storyboard, inspired by how Disney planned Snow White:
15 frames for the guest and 15 for the host, drawn by an animator from Pixar. When the team put every
idea on sticky notes under the frames, most ideas sat on the website. The offline moments, which
matter more, had almost none. That gap led to products such as the Neighborhood Guides, and teams were
organised by frame.
