## Why it matters for a product engineer

A product has a network effect when each new user makes it more valuable for the others: a
marketplace with more sellers, a chat app with more friends on it. NFX estimates that network effects
explain 70% of the value created in technology since 1994. They are also why such products are hard to
start: the first users get the least value. Engineers build the parts that make the network visible
and useful from the first few users.

## How to do it

1. Name the type of network: users connected to users, buyers to sellers, or a platform with
   developers. Each grows differently.
2. Find the smallest network that works on its own: one team, one city, one class.
3. Make that small network useful before you expand: seed it with content, supply or invitations.
4. Measure each network, not the total. Value comes from density, not size.
5. For marketplaces, watch both sides and keep them in balance.
6. Watch for negative effects: spam, crowding and noise as the network grows.

## Common mistakes

- Calling any growth a network effect. Many products get no better with more users.
- Launching everywhere at once, so no network gets dense enough.
- Total user counts that hide empty networks.
- Ignoring the side of a marketplace that is harder to attract.

## Example

At Uber, Andrew Chen saw the weekly review go through the business city by city, and even district by
district. Each city was its own network, with riders on one side and drivers on the other. Totals such
as all trips worldwide were treated as "mostly meaningless". What mattered was the balance in each
city: too much surge and riders left, too little and drivers went home.
