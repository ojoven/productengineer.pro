## Why it matters for a product engineer

Latency is what people feel as speed. An average hides the slow requests, and the slow requests are
the ones people remember. A product engineer owns the whole path of a request, from the tap to the
database and back, so they can see where the time goes and decide which part is worth making faster.

## How to do it

1. Measure percentiles, not averages: p50 for the usual case, p95 and p99 for the slow tail.
2. Measure where the person waits. A fast server behind a slow network or a slow render is still
   slow.
3. Estimate before you build. Sending 32 KiB to a server in the same cloud zone takes about 250 μs.
   Sending 1 MiB across the Atlantic takes about 100 ms. A design with many sequential calls to a
   distant service cannot be fast.
4. Split a slow request into its parts: network, server work, database and rendering. Fix the
   largest part first.
5. Look at the slowest requests one by one. A tail often has a cause the average never shows, such
   as one busy key or a garbage collection pause.
6. Set a target for each important action, and alert when its percentile goes above the target.

## Common mistakes

- Reporting the average, or only the median.
- Measuring the server and calling it the person's wait.
- Many calls in a row, where one call or parallel calls would do.
- Averaging the p99 of several servers. Compute a percentile from all the requests.
- Making a part faster when it is a small share of the total.

## Example

Discord stored its messages in Cassandra, on 177 nodes by early 2022. When many people read one busy
channel, its partition became "hot". Reads and writes needed a quorum of nodes, so every query to
those nodes became slower, not only the busy channel's. Garbage collection pauses added more spikes.
In May 2022 Discord moved the messages to ScyllaDB, behind new services that combine identical
requests.
