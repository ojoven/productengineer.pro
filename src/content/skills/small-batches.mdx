## Why it matters for a product engineer

A small change is easy to review, test, release and undo. It also brings feedback sooner, while you
still remember why you made it. Research on software delivery links small batches to both speed and
stability. For a product engineer, small batches turn a roadmap into a steady flow of things users can
try.

## How to do it

1. Split work so each piece is useful or testable on its own and can ship in a day or two.
2. Ship the parts users do not see first: the data model, the API, the job. Turn the feature on later
   with a flag.
3. Keep each pull request to one purpose.
4. Release each piece when it is ready, instead of waiting for the whole feature.
5. Ask for feedback on each piece, and let it change the next one.
6. Watch the size of your changes and the time from start to production. If they grow, split more.

## Common mistakes

- Splitting by layer (all the database work, then all the interface), so nothing is usable until the
  end.
- Small pull requests that wait days for review.
- Releasing small pieces but announcing nothing until the big launch.
- A feature branch that lives for weeks.

## Example

Papermark started on weekends and nights. Today it ships to production almost every day. The
milestone post for $2M ARR listed more than 20 releases, and the changelog post for October 2026
counts a hundred small improvements next to the bigger features. The big steps toward data rooms for
deals came as a series of releases. ([Papermark in In practice](/in-practice/papermark/))
