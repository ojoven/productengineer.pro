## Why it matters for a product engineer

When something breaks, users want to know three things: what happened, what you are doing, and what
they should do. Silence makes them guess, and they usually guess the worst. A short, honest update
keeps trust even during a bad outage. Product engineers often know the facts first, so they can give
the people who write updates accurate words quickly.

## How to do it

1. Post a first status update within minutes, even if it only says that you are investigating.
2. Update on a fixed rhythm, such as every 30 minutes, and say when the next update will come.
3. Write in plain words: who is affected, what works, what does not, and any workaround.
4. Name one person to write updates, so engineers can focus on the fix.
5. After the incident, publish what happened, why, and what will change. Apologize without excuses.

## Common mistakes

- A status page that shows "all systems operational" during an outage.
- Technical terms that users cannot act on.
- No update for hours because there is "nothing new".
- Blaming a vendor or a customer instead of saying what you will change.

## Example

On 8 June 2021, a valid configuration change by one Fastly customer triggered a hidden bug, and 85% of
Fastly's network returned errors. Fastly's monitoring detected it within a minute, and the first status
post came 11 minutes after the start. Within 49 minutes, 95% of the network was working normally. The
same day, Fastly published a summary with the cause, a timeline, an apology and the changes it would
make. It wrote: "we should have anticipated it".
