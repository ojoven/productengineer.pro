## Why it matters for a product engineer

A problem statement makes the team agree on what it is solving before it argues about how. For an
engineer it is the test of done that a ticket does not give: after we ship, is this problem smaller?
It also limits scope, because every new idea must make the same problem smaller.

## How to do it

1. Answer the five Ws: who is affected, what the problem is, where and when it happens, and why it
   happens and matters.
2. Write the background: who has the problem and why it started. If you do not know the cause yet,
   say so. Finding it is the work of discovery.
3. Name every group it affects, including internal staff such as support.
4. State the impact on the organisation: cost, revenue or reputation. Put a number on it when you can.
5. Leave the solution out.
6. Keep one problem per statement. Write it early, and add to it as research brings facts.

## Common mistakes

- A solution written as a problem: "users need a dashboard".
- A list of unrelated problems in one statement.
- No impact, so nobody can judge whether the problem is worth solving.
- Writing it once and never checking the shipped work against it.

## Example

One of NN/g's examples is about a contact centre. Applicants call for an update on their application,
wait a long time, and the staff cannot answer them. Nothing in the statement says "build a tracking
page", so the team is still free to find the best fix.
