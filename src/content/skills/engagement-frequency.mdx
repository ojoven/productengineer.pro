## Why it matters for a product engineer

Needs come back at different speeds. People message friends every day, plan work on weekdays and file
taxes once a year. A metric built for daily use makes a weekly product look weak and a yearly product
look dead. A product engineer who knows how often the need comes back can choose the right window for
the metric, and find real problems instead of false alarms.

## How to do it

1. Write down how often the need behind the product comes back: every day, on workdays, every week,
   every month or every year.
2. Choose an active-user window that matches it: a day, a week, a month or a season.
3. Count the active days of each user in that window, and plot the distribution, not only an average.
4. Compare cohorts and segments over time. In a healthy product, more users move toward the frequent
   end of the curve.
5. Talk to users who come back less often than the need does. They may solve the problem somewhere
   else.

## Common mistakes

- Using DAU/MAU for a product that is not meant for daily use.
- Comparing your frequency with products that serve a different need.
- Using notifications to push people to come back more often than the need.
- Counting app opens instead of the action that delivers value.

## Example

Andreessen Horowitz describes the power user curve: a histogram of how many days each user was active
in a month. Facebook's growth team called it L30. For a social product, the healthy shape is a smile,
with a large group active nearly every day. For a work product, the authors suggest a 7-day version,
L7. There, the curve can smile through 5 days and drop at 6 and 7, because people do not work at
weekends. They note that DAU/MAU is not the right metric for such a product.
