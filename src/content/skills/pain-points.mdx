## Why it matters for a product engineer

A pain point is a moment where work costs too much: time, money, risk or effort. A feature that starts
from a real pain point has people waiting for it. A feature that starts from a team idea still has to
find them. Engineers often see pain points first, in support tickets, error logs and the workarounds
people build.

## How to do it

1. Collect moments, not opinions. Ask "the last time this happened, what did you do?"
2. Look for effort people already spend: a spreadsheet, copy and paste, a second tool, a call to
   support. A workaround is evidence that the pain is real.
3. Name what people use today, even when it is not a product, and where each option fails.
4. Write each pain point as one moment: who has it, when, and what it costs them.
5. Place the pain points under your outcome as opportunities, as in Teresa Torres's opportunity
   solution tree, and compare them before you choose solutions.
6. Rank them by [severity and frequency](/skills-map/discovery/problem-identification/problem-severity/).

## Common mistakes

- Feature requests written as pain points, such as "there is no export button".
- Counting complaints instead of the people who have the problem.
- Pain points that appear only after you suggested them.
- Fixing the loudest customer's pain first.

## Example

An agent's audit of RelievApp judged the problem before the product. It listed where today's
alternatives fail, and scored the problem 8 out of 10 as worth solving. The product's answer scored 5,
because the route to a session was slow. A strong pain point does not make a product good. It makes it
worth improving.
