## Why it matters for a product engineer

A product retains when the problem it solves comes back. If people need it every week, good retention
is possible. If they need it once a year, no amount of reminders will make them daily users. Knowing
how often the need returns sets honest targets, and tells you whether to design for habits or for
coming back after a long break.

## How to do it

1. Write the problem in the user's words, and ask how often it happens without your product.
2. Check it in the data: the power user curve, a histogram of active days per month, shows the real
   rhythm.
3. Set retention targets at that rhythm: weekly for a weekly need, yearly for a yearly one.
4. For rare needs, keep the account alive between uses: saved work, a pause instead of a
   cancellation, a reminder when the need is likely to return.
5. Look for a second, more frequent need next to the first. That is often how products grow.

## Common mistakes

- Daily targets for a product people need once a month.
- Reminders that create a reason to open the app, but no need.
- Treating a quiet period as churn, when the need simply has not returned.
- Adding features for a frequent need that your users do not have.

## Example

Papermark and Zernio sell to similar customers, but their needs return at very different rhythms. A
data room lasts as long as a fundraise or a sale. Zernio's customers need it again with every new
platform feature, because each one means another review or quota. Each product's design follows the
rhythm. ([Papermark](/in-practice/papermark/), [Zernio](/in-practice/zernio/))
