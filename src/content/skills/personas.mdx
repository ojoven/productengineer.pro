## Why it matters for a product engineer

Personas give a team a shared picture of who it builds for, so a discussion about a feature can name
a real kind of user. For engineers they are most useful when they connect to data. When the analytics
can assign every user to a persona, the team can see how each group behaves.

## How to do it

1. Start from research, not demographics: several
   [interviews](/skills-map/discovery/user-research/user-interviews/) for each type of user you
   expect to find.
2. Group people by goals, context and behaviour. Age and job title rarely explain what someone needs.
3. Turn each group into a short profile: the goal, the context, the main frustration, the tools they
   use, and one quote from the research.
4. Write simple rules that assign a real user to a persona from product data. Check the rules with more
   interviews.
5. Share the personas where decisions happen: planning, design reviews and the dashboards.
6. Keep between three and six. With more, nobody remembers them.

## Common mistakes

- Personas invented in a workshop, with no research behind them.
- Details that change no decision, such as hobbies or a stock photo.
- So many personas that each one stands for nobody.
- Never updating them after the product or its users change.

## Example

On Spotify's internal analytics platform, "data practitioner" was too broad to guide the product. The
team combined interviews with data on how employees used the tools, and found six personas, from
Paola the Product Strategist to Dalia the Data Dabbler. Simple rules assign each person, such as
"Employee has used dashboarding tools more than 90% of data scientists". A daily dataset classifies
every internal user, so the team can split its key results by persona.
