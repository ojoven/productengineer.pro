## Why it matters for a product engineer

The analysis is where an experiment becomes a decision, and where most mistakes become decisions
too. A product engineer who checks the data, the size of the effect and the guardrails before
deciding avoids shipping noise. Writing the reasoning down lets others check it.

## How to do it

1. Check that the test is valid first: the sample ratio, the logging, and the planned duration.
2. Read the effect with its interval, not only the p-value.
3. Check the guardrail metrics and the key segments.
4. Be suspicious of surprising results. Twyman's law says that any figure that looks interesting or
   different is usually wrong. Look for a bug, then repeat the test.
5. Write down the decision, the reasons, and what result would have changed it.

## Common mistakes

- Reading a p-value of 0.05 as a 95% chance that the change works.
- Trusting "observed power" calculated after the test.
- Sharing a big win from a test with a few hundred users.
- Stopping the test when the result looks good.

## Example

In 2021, a newsletter shared an A/B test of two landing pages with the title "Which design radically
increased conversions 337%?". The p-value was 0.009. Ron Kohavi, Alex Deng and Lukas Vermeer used it
as their first example of a result not to trust. There were about 80 users per variant. To detect
even a 10% change, this test needed about 41,642 users per variant, so its power was about 3%. After
feedback, the site added that the experiment was underpowered.
