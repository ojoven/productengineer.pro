## Why it matters for a product engineer

A control group shows what would have happened without the change. Without one, a season, a
marketing campaign, a bug fix or simply what people believe can look like a result. For a product
engineer, the control group is what turns "the number went up after we shipped" into "the change
moved the number".

## How to do it

1. Decide which unit gets the change, such as a user or an account, and keep a group of the same
   units without it.
2. Assign units to the groups by chance, not by who volunteers.
3. Measure both groups in the same way, over the same period.
4. For long-term effects, keep a small holdout group without new features for months. GrowthBook
   lists the costs: those users miss improvements, and the feature flags must stay in the code for
   the whole period.
5. Measure behaviour in both groups. What people report about themselves is not a control.

## Common mistakes

- Comparing before and after a launch, as if nothing else changed.
- Using the people who chose not to use a feature as the control.
- A holdout group that receives the new features through another path.
- Ending the holdout before the long-term effect has time to show.

## Example

In 2025, METR ran a randomized trial with 16 experienced open-source developers, on 246 real issues
in their own repositories. Each issue was randomly assigned to allow AI tools or not, so the issues
without AI were the control. With AI, the developers took 19% longer. Before the study, they expected
AI to make them 24% faster. Afterwards, they still believed it had made them 20% faster. METR says
these results describe early-2025 tools, and it has since published newer data.
