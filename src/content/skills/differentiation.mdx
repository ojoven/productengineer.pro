## Why it matters for a product engineer

Differentiation is the reason someone picks your product instead of another one. It often comes from
an engineering choice: a different architecture, a simpler integration, a lower cost to run. Engineers
who know where the product should differ put their best work there, and keep the rest simply good
enough.

## How to do it

1. List what customers would use if your product did not exist, including doing nothing.
2. For each one, write what your product does differently, in facts: faster, cheaper, fewer steps, a
   capability the others lack.
3. Keep only the differences your target customers care about. Ask them, or watch what they choose.
4. Check what must be at least equal. Buyers rarely switch for one strength if a basic feature is
   missing.
5. Put the difference where people see it: the first screen, the demo, the pricing page.
6. Check it again when competitors change. A difference that everyone copies becomes a minimum.

## Common mistakes

- Being different in ways nobody values.
- Differentiating on features before reaching the basics buyers expect.
- Claims like "easy" or "powerful" that every competitor also makes.
- Hiding the difference in the documentation, where buyers never look.

## Example

Papermark first matched the features buyers expected from DocSend and data room tools. Then it chose
where to differ: a custom domain by default, and rooms that carry the customer's brand where the
incumbents show their own. Mamitsu differs in how it works: the model gets the raw video, not a
transcript, and the answer comes back in the same request. RelievApp differs in the form: eyes open,
hands on, two minutes. ([Papermark](/in-practice/papermark/), [Mamitsu](/in-practice/mamitsu/),
[RelievApp](/in-practice/relievapp-part-1/))
