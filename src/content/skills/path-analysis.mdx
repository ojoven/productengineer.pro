## Why it matters for a product engineer

Designers draw one journey. People take many: they arrive from search, go back, loop and take
detours. Path analysis shows the routes they really follow, so a product engineer can add a shortcut
where people already go, and fix the place where they go in circles.

## How to do it

1. Choose a point to study: a goal, such as a signup, or an entry, such as a landing page.
2. Look at the most common paths into it and out of it, with a Sankey diagram or a process map.
3. Then define the journey you expect, and count how many people follow it.
4. Look for loops, back-and-forth between two pages, and repeated use of the same navigation.
5. Split by device and by entry point. A path that works on desktop can fail on a phone.
6. Change one thing, such as a direct link, and compare the paths afterwards.

## Common mistakes

- Reading the designed journey as the real one.
- Paths so detailed that no two are the same, so nothing is common.
- Assuming a long path is bad. Sometimes people are comparing options on purpose.
- Changing navigation for one path and breaking others.

## Example

GOV.UK's analysts studied journeys into the step-by-step guide for setting up a limited company.
They used two methods: a process map of the most common journeys, and a defined journey counted
with an open-source tool. Both showed that 20% of users went through the same three pages before
reaching the guide. They also found people bouncing between the limited company and sole trader
content, a sign of confusion about which one they needed.
