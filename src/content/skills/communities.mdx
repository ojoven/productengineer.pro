## Why it matters for a product engineer

Communities are where users help each other, recommend products and say what is missing. Joining the
ones your users already belong to, and being useful there, often brings the first users. A product's
own community can become support, documentation and a source of ideas. Engineers are welcome in most
technical communities, as long as they help rather than advertise.

## How to do it

1. Find where your users already talk: forums, chat groups, open-source projects, local meetups.
2. Take part as a person: answer questions, share what you learned, and mention the product only
   where it helps.
3. Join communities of other makers too. Founders at the same stage share users, channels and advice.
4. Start your own community only when users already want to talk to each other.
5. Give contributors real work: issues labelled for newcomers, translations, docs.
6. Expect the community to change as the product grows, and change what you ask of it.

## Common mistakes

- Joining a community only to post links.
- A forum or chat group with nobody from the team in it.
- Counting members instead of people who help each other.
- Asking for the same contributions when the product has outgrown them.

## Example

Papermark's first 1,000 GitHub stars came in its first couple of months, from viral posts, Hacktoberfest
and a circle of commercial open-source founders: "we had each other's back". Later, outside code
contributions fell as the codebase grew, and AI tools took over much of the work casual contributors
used to do. ([Papermark in In practice](/in-practice/papermark/))
