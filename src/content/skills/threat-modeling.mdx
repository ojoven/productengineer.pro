## Why it matters for a product engineer

Security problems found in design cost minutes; found in production they cost incidents, trust and
weekends. Threat modeling is a short, structured conversation about how a feature could be attacked or
misused. A product engineer is often the one who knows the data flows well enough to lead it.

## How to do it

1. Draw what you are building: the parts, the data, who talks to whom, and where trust changes, such
   as between the browser and the server.
2. Ask what can go wrong at each crossing. STRIDE is a useful checklist: spoofing, tampering,
   repudiation, information disclosure, denial of service, elevation of privilege.
3. Think about misuse by real users too, not only attackers.
4. Decide what you will do about each threat: fix, reduce, accept or transfer it.
5. Turn the decisions into tasks and tests, with an owner.
6. Ask whether you did a good enough job, and repeat when the design changes.

## Common mistakes

- Doing it once, at the end, as a document for an audit.
- A long list of threats with no decisions.
- Only thinking about outside attackers, not about users, partners or insiders.
- Missing the data flows to third parties, such as analytics or AI providers.

## Example

Papermark's buyers share documents they cannot afford to leak. Its protections read like the answers
to a threat model. A forwarded link: email verification. A copied page: screenshot protection. A leak
with no trace: dynamic watermarks with the viewer's details. Someone reading without a legal duty: an
NDA before access. A stolen session that takes everything: one-time codes for bulk downloads.
([Papermark in In practice](/in-practice/papermark/))
