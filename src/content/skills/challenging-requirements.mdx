## Why it matters for a product engineer

A requirement often arrives as a solution: "add a calendar", "export to Excel", "send a notification".
Behind it there is a problem that nobody wrote down. If you build the solution as asked, you may build
something big that solves the problem badly. If you find the problem first, the answer is often
smaller and better. Product engineers see the cost of each option, so they are in a good position to
ask the question, as long as they ask it to help, not to block.

## How to do it

1. Ask about the moment of need: "When did you need this? What were you trying to do?"
2. Write the problem in one sentence, separate from the requested solution.
3. Check how many people have the problem, and how they solve it today.
4. Propose the smallest solution that solves that problem, with its cost.
5. Agree on the problem with the person who asked before you build anything.

## Common mistakes

- Building exactly what was asked because it is faster than asking.
- Asking "why?" in a way that sounds like a refusal.
- Asking people to describe the solution they want instead of what happened.
- Challenging every request, including small, clear ones.

## Example

Soon after Basecamp 3 launched without a calendar, customers asked for one. A full calendar could take
six months or more, and only about 10% of customers had used the calendars in past versions. So the
team called a customer and asked when she had wanted a calendar. She had driven to her office to check
a wall calendar for free meeting rooms. The real need was to see free days. The team built the Dot
Grid, a much smaller calendar, in a six-week cycle.
