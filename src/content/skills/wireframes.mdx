## Why it matters for a product engineer

A wireframe shows what goes on a screen and roughly where, without colours or final copy. It keeps
the discussion on structure and flow, when changing them still costs minutes. For an engineer, it is
also the earliest view of the states, data and components a feature needs.

## How to do it

1. Start from one task and the content it needs, not from a layout you like.
2. Draw at the lowest detail that answers the question: boxes and labels on paper, or a thick pen on a
   tablet.
3. Show every state that matters: empty, loading, full, error.
4. Use real words for labels and actions where you can. Placeholder text hides problems.
5. Walk through the task with someone else, screen by screen. Change the wireframe, not the code.
6. Turn it into a short list of elements the solution must have, and leave the visual design open.

## Common mistakes

- Adding colour, images and polish too early, so feedback goes to the looks.
- Wireframing only the main screen, not the steps around it.
- Treating the wireframe as the final design, so designers have no room left.
- Lorem ipsum where the real words would change the layout.

## Example

Basecamp's users made fake to-dos such as "––– Needs testing –––" to divide their lists. The team
sketched an official divider with a thick marker, broad enough that adding detail was impossible. The
sketch fixed the elements: loose to-dos above the first group, groups below. Where the add button
would go stayed open, and the visual design was left to the designers.
