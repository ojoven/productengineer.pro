## Why it matters for a product engineer

Market sizing tells you whether an idea can pay for a team or only for a hobby. A product engineer
does not need a perfect number. You need a rough one, built from assumptions you can see, so you can
decide how much time the idea deserves and which customers to build for first.

## How to do it

1. Describe the customer precisely, using your
   [ideal customer profile](/skills-map/discovery/user-understanding/ideal-customer-profile/).
2. Count them from real sources: public registers, app store data, job posts, member lists of
   associations, the customer counts of competitors.
3. Multiply by a realistic price per year, taken from your pricing or from what people pay today.
   That gives a bottom-up estimate.
4. Narrow it to the customers you can reach in the next two or three years, with your channels and
   languages.
5. Check it against a top-down number and against competitors' revenue. If the numbers are far apart,
   find out why.
6. Write the assumptions next to the result, and update it when you learn more.

## Common mistakes

- "If we get 1% of this huge market…". A share of a big number proves nothing.
- A market defined far too broadly, such as "everyone with a phone".
- Sizing only today's category, when the product can change how people behave.
- False precision: three significant digits on a rough guess.
- Counting users who will never pay.

## Example

In June 2014, the finance professor Aswath Damodaran valued Uber at $5.9 billion. He assumed a global
taxi and limousine market of $100 billion and a maximum share of 10% for Uber. Bill Gurley, an Uber
investor and board member, answered that the market was wrong, not the math: cars that arrive in
minutes, cover the suburbs and need no cash create new trips. In San Francisco, he wrote, the new
market already looked about three times the old taxi market.
