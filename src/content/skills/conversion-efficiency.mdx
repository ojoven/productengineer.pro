## Why it matters for a product engineer

Each channel brings people with a different intent, at a different cost. A channel that brings many
visitors who never activate can cost more per customer than a small channel with strong conversion.
The full path from first visit to paying customer, for each channel, shows where money and effort
bring the most customers. Often the cheapest improvement is a weak step in the product, not more
traffic.

## How to do it

1. Tag every signup with its channel, and keep the tag through activation and payment.
2. Calculate the conversion between each pair of stages, for each channel.
3. Calculate the cost per paying customer for each channel, and compare it with what a customer pays.
4. Find the stage with the largest loss in your best channels, and improve it first.
5. Recalculate after each change, because a gain in one stage can lower quality in the next.

## Common mistakes

- Comparing channels by cost per signup, not cost per paying customer.
- Losing the channel tag between signup and payment.
- Changing the product for one channel without checking the others.
- Averages across channels that hide a channel that loses money.

## Example

In early 2009, Dropbox tried paid search. People who arrived from ads got a time-limited trial instead
of the free account. The cost per acquisition was $233 to $388, for a product that cost $99. Dropbox
then built a referral program with a reward for both sides, which permanently increased signups by
60%. By early 2010, 35% of daily signups came from referrals, and 20% from shared folders and other
viral features.
