## Why it matters for a product engineer

Progressive disclosure keeps the main path simple and moves rare or advanced options one step away.
One product can then serve beginners and experts at the same time. NN/g says it improves three of
usability's five components: learnability, efficiency of use and error rate. Engineers implement the
split, and often know from the data which options people really use.

## How to do it

1. List every option on the screen, and find out how often each one is used: from analytics, research
   or support questions.
2. Show the few options most people need. What appears first tells people it is important.
3. Move the rest behind one clear control, such as "More options", with a label that says what is
   there.
4. Keep it to two levels. If people must open a third, the split is wrong.
5. When options belong to separate steps, use staged disclosure instead: one step at a time, as in a
   wizard.
6. Check the split with real use, and move an option up when many people go looking for it.

## Common mistakes

- Hiding an option that many people need, so they cannot find it.
- A vague label such as "Advanced" that does not say what is behind it.
- Several layers of "More", so people get lost.
- Deciding what counts as advanced from the team's opinion, not from research.

## Example

RelievApp's first session used to show guide cards, a thumb hold, a choice of length and a picker
before the first breath. After an agent's audit, the first run became Start, one question, Continue,
and the first breath. The other steps were taken out of the first run, so a first-time user meets one
decision, not five. ([RelievApp in In practice](/in-practice/relievapp-part-1/))
