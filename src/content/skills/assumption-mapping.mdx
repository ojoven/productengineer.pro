## Why it matters for a product engineer

Every idea rests on beliefs that may be false: people want it, can use it, will pay for it, and the
team can build it. Mapping those beliefs shows the risk before the build. For an engineer, the map
decides what to build first: the cheapest test of the riskiest belief, not the most interesting part
of the code.

## How to do it

1. Walk through the idea step by step, as in a story map, and write what must be true at each step.
2. Check that the list covers Teresa Torres's five types: desirability, usability, feasibility,
   viability and ethics. Do not argue about which type an assumption is; the list is what matters.
3. Place each assumption on two scales from David Bland: how important it is to success, and how much
   evidence you already have.
4. Test the important assumptions with little evidence first, with the smallest test that can give
   evidence.
5. Write down what success looks like before the test, so nobody can read the result to fit.
6. Move each assumption on the map as evidence arrives.

## Common mistakes

- Listing only desirability, and forgetting feasibility, viability and ethics.
- Placing assumptions by feeling and calling it evidence.
- Testing the easy assumptions because they are easy.
- A map made once at the start and never updated.

## Example

Mamitsu's maker has evidence for one belief: Supadata's traction shows that people pay to get data
out of videos. The belief that matters most has no evidence yet: that they will pay Mamitsu, which is
several times cheaper. The relaunch is the test.
