## Why it matters for a product engineer

Every feature keeps costing time after launch: tests, bug fixes, support questions, security work.
Some features are no longer worth that cost, and some are a risk. Removing them makes the product and
the code simpler. The hard part is the people who still use them. Product engineers can find exactly
who those people are in the data, and build a path that moves them before anything breaks.

## How to do it

1. Use data to find who still uses the feature, how often, and through which clients.
2. Announce a date, the reason, and the replacement, with a few months' notice.
3. Contact the affected users directly, and warn them in the product when they use the feature.
4. Before the final date, switch the feature off for short, announced periods, so people notice.
5. Remove it on the date, and keep a clear error message that explains what to do next.

## Common mistakes

- Removing a feature because it looks unused, without checking who uses it.
- An announcement in a blog post that the affected users never read.
- Moving the date again and again, so nobody believes it.
- An error after removal that does not say what changed or what to do.

## Example

GitHub stopped accepting account passwords for Git operations on 13 August 2021, because tokens are
safer: they can be limited, revoked and generated per device. It announced the plan in July 2020 and
the date in December 2020. It emailed users who still used passwords. On 30 June and 28 July 2021, it
blocked passwords for two three-hour periods each day. People whose scripts failed in those windows
had time to switch to a token or an SSH key.
