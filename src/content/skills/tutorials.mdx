## Why it matters for a product engineer

A good tutorial teaches by doing, with the user's own data or a realistic task. A bad one is a
slideshow that people skip and forget. For a product engineer, the measure is not whether people
finish the tutorial, but whether they then succeed on their own.

## How to do it

1. Use a tutorial only when the interaction is new to most users. For familiar patterns, use
   contextual help.
2. Make people perform each step, not watch it.
3. Use their real task or data when possible.
4. Keep the help available after the tutorial, at the place where people need it.
5. Let people skip the tutorial, and come back to it later.
6. Measure task success after the tutorial, and compare it with users who skipped it.

## Common mistakes

- A tour of the interface instead of a task.
- Counting tutorial completion as success.
- Dropping people into the product with nothing after the last step.
- Assuming people know what kind of product it is. NN/g saw new users of AI chatbots who did not know
  the interface was a conversation.

## Example

NN/g describes the Gates Carbon Drive app, which checks the tension of a bike's belt. You pluck the
belt like a guitar string, and the app measures the sound through the phone's microphone. A new
interaction like this is a good reason for a tutorial, and this one made users perform the task, not
only watch it. But when the tutorial ended, the app dropped users into the main screen with no
further guidance, so they had to remember the steps. They could open the tutorial again later.
