## Why it matters for a product engineer

New users do not need every feature on the first day, and they forget what a long tour shows them.
Progressive onboarding teaches each feature when people reach it, so the lesson arrives with the
need. It depends on usage data: the product must know what each person has already done.

## How to do it

1. Order the features by when most users need them, using your usage data.
2. Attach a short hint to the first use of each feature, not to the first launch of the product.
3. Show one hint at a time, with a visual and very little text.
4. Store which hints each user has seen or dismissed, across devices, and do not repeat them.
5. Measure whether people use the feature after the hint, not how many saw the hint.

## Common mistakes

- A five-screen tour at first launch.
- Several hints in a row, so people dismiss all of them.
- Hints that cover the control they explain.
- Hint state stored on one device, so people see the same tips again on another.

## Example

NN/g compared two approaches in mobile apps. The Makr iPad app showed a series of text tips before
people could use the tool, so they had to read and remember every step first. People who tapped
through quickly reached the workspace with no idea how to continue. The YouTube Android app showed
hints one at a time, as the user reached the relevant section, and each hint was about one element
of the interface.
