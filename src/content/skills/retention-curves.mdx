## Why it matters for a product engineer

A retention curve is the most honest test of whether a product works. Product engineers ship changes
every week, and the curve of each new cohort shows whether those changes help people stay. Where the
curve becomes flat, and at what level, says more than any single retention number.

## How to do it

1. Define "active" as a real value action, such as sending a message or publishing a post, not a
   login.
2. Group users by the week or month they started. For each group, plot the share that is still
   active in each later period.
3. Choose the period from how often people naturally need the product: daily for a chat app, monthly
   for an accounting tool, yearly for a home search.
4. Read the shape first. Does it become flat? When, and at what level? Then compare new cohorts with
   old ones. Newer curves above older ones mean the product is getting better.
5. Compare your level only with products like yours: the same kind of customer, price and natural
   frequency.
6. Split a cohort by channel, plan or first action to see which users stay.

## Common mistakes

- Counting a login or an app open as "active".
- Comparing your curve with another kind of product. Products for small businesses, consumer apps
  and marketplaces retain at very different levels.
- Judging too early. Some curves take more than six months to become flat.
- Cohorts that are too broad, or one average line for all users, which hides the effect of a change
  shipped in the middle of the month.

## Example

When a16z studied revenue retention from dozens of AI companies, most curves fell fast at first, as
"AI tourists" tried the product and left, then became flat around month 3. ChatGPT's curve went up
again later, a "smile": people who had left or used it less came back as the product improved. So
a16z suggests reading month 12 divided by month 3, not only retention from the first month.
