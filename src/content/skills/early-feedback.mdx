## Why it matters for a product engineer

The earlier you hear a real reaction, the cheaper it is to change direction. A comment on a rough demo
costs an hour to act on; the same comment after launch costs a quarter. Engineers control when work
becomes visible, so the habit of showing unfinished work is theirs to build.

## How to do it

1. Decide what you want to learn from each piece of work, and the earliest version that could teach
   it.
2. Show it before it feels ready: a demo, a recording, an internal release, a link to a few users.
3. Put the work where feedback arrives by itself: a public post, a beta group, a changelog.
4. Ask specific questions, such as "would you use this in your next deal?", not "what do you think?".
5. Write down what you heard and what you changed because of it.
6. Repeat with each piece, so feedback becomes a rhythm and not a single launch event.

## Common mistakes

- Waiting for polish before showing anything.
- Asking only colleagues, who share your assumptions.
- Collecting feedback and never changing the plan.
- One big launch as the first contact with users.

## Example

Papermark's first months were weekends and nights, shared in public even when unfinished. Marc Seitz
wrote six posts on dev.to about how he built each part. When Papermark launched on Product Hunt on 4
September 2023, it had weeks of feedback behind it: "the launch is the harvest, not the seed", he
wrote. ([Papermark in In practice](/in-practice/papermark/))
