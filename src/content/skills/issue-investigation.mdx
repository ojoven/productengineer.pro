## Why it matters for a product engineer

A user report says what someone saw, not what happened. Investigation connects the report to logs,
traces and data until the problem can be reproduced. Product engineers are well placed for this,
because they know both the code and the product. Often the answer is not a bug at all, but a screen
that let the user believe something untrue.

## How to do it

1. Get the facts from the user: what they did, what they expected, what happened, and when.
2. Find the same moment in your systems: the account, the request, the trace, the error.
3. Reproduce it with the same data and steps. Until you can, it is a theory.
4. Ask whether it is a real error, whether it affects other users, and who needs to know.
5. Decide where the fix belongs: the code, the data, the docs or the design.
6. Tell the user what you found, and add what was missing to your logs or admin tools.

## Common mistakes

- Assuming the user did something wrong.
- Investigating without the user's exact time and account, and searching all logs by guesswork.
- Fixing the data by hand without finding why it went wrong.
- Closing the ticket without telling the user.

## Example

At Mamitsu, an agent does the first part of every investigation. For each new error, it opens a
Slack thread and works out whether the error is real and whether its maker needs to look. Papermark
keeps most of its issues public on GitHub, so customers can watch their bug being fixed.
([Mamitsu](/in-practice/mamitsu/), [Papermark](/in-practice/papermark/))
