## Why it matters for a product engineer

A demo should show the prospect's own problem being solved, in a few minutes, not a tour of every
feature. That needs a demo environment with realistic data, which is product work. And engineers who
can demo well are valuable in sales conversations: they can answer the hard question on the spot,
and they hear the objections first.

## How to do it

1. Learn the prospect's problem before the demo: who they are, what they use today, what the
   decision depends on.
2. Show one story, from their problem to the result, with data that looks like theirs.
3. Build a demo environment with realistic sample data, kept separate from real customers.
4. Let them ask and interrupt. Their questions show what matters to them.
5. End with a next step they can take: a trial, a pilot, a second call with their team.
6. Write down what they asked and where they hesitated, and share it with the product team.

## Common mistakes

- Showing every feature.
- Empty accounts, or test data with names like "asdf".
- Demos that only work on the presenter's laptop.
- Answering "yes, it can do that" to questions you have not checked.

## Example

Papermark's home page does a demo without a call: a workspace with data rooms named like real deals,
visitors with view times, and a chart of time spent per page for two versions of a document. Mamitsu's
maker, who used to struggle to explain his ideas, now often builds a two-hour demo and sends that
instead. ([Papermark](/in-practice/papermark/), [Mamitsu](/in-practice/mamitsu/))
