## Why it matters for a product engineer

Feedback arrives as bug reports, feature requests, questions and complaints, often mixed in one
message. Without triage it piles up, and the useful parts are lost. Triage decides what each item
becomes: a bug, a request, a duplicate, a docs fix, or nothing. A clear, public process also tells
users what will happen to their request. Product engineers can triage fast because they know what is a
bug and what the product was designed to do.

## How to do it

1. Look at every new item within a fixed time, such as one working day.
2. Label it by type and area, and send it to the owner of that area.
3. Close what you will not do, with a short reason: duplicate, works as designed, out of scope.
4. Group requests, and count the people and customers behind each one.
5. When something ships, tell the people who asked for it.

## Common mistakes

- Leaving requests open for years, which is a silent "no".
- Triage by one person, who becomes a bottleneck.
- Counting votes only, without asking who votes and why.
- Closing items with no explanation.

## Example

The Visual Studio Code team publishes its triage process. A new issue is usually triaged within about
one business day. It can be closed as a duplicate, as designed, or for lack of information after 7
days. A feature request that the team plans within 24 months goes to the backlog. Others go to a
community review list. If one gets more than 20 up-votes, a bot moves it to the backlog. If it stays on
the list for more than 60 days, the bot closes it.
