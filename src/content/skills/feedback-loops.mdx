## Why it matters for a product engineer

Many product metrics feed back into themselves. More users bring more content, which brings more
users: a reinforcing loop, and the reason some products grow faster and faster. Other loops balance:
more load brings slower pages, which bring fewer visits. When a metric speeds up or stops for no clear
reason, a loop is often the cause. Product engineers who can see the loop know where to act, and which
fixes will only push the problem around.

## How to do it

1. Draw the metric you care about, and ask what it causes, and what causes it.
2. Follow the arrows until one comes back to the start. That is a loop.
3. Mark each loop as reinforcing (it grows itself) or balancing (it limits itself).
4. Look for delays: a loop with a long delay often surprises people.
5. Change the part of the loop you control, and watch the whole loop, not one number.

## Common mistakes

- Treating each metric as separate when they drive each other.
- Expecting a growth loop to continue forever without a limit.
- Fixing one symptom of a balancing loop, so the pressure moves somewhere else.
- Forgetting your own response is part of the loop.

## Example

English Wikipedia grew in a reinforcing loop: a more valuable encyclopedia attracted more editors. It
peaked in March 2007 at 56,400 active editors, then declined. Researchers found that the community's
own response to growth was part of the cause. To fight vandalism, editors used fast tools and bots,
which also rejected the work of good-faith newcomers. By mid-2008, 75% of new users got their first
message from a tool. Rejected newcomers were less likely to stay.
