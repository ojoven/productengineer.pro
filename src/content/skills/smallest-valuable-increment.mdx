## Why it matters for a product engineer

A layer of the stack is not something a user can use. The smallest valuable increment is: something a
real user notices and uses, however small. It starts feedback while changes are still cheap, and it
keeps the plan easy to change. Engineers find it by asking, at each step, what a user can do now that
they could not do before.

## How to do it

1. Describe the problem and whom it affects. Then ask, as Henrik Kniberg does, "What is your
   skateboard?": the smallest thing you can put in the hands of real users.
2. Cut along the problem, not along the code: one region, one type of customer, one kind of case.
3. Make each increment usable on its own, even if users still do the rest the old way.
4. Give it to real users who know it is early, and collect what they say and do.
5. Add the next case or region only when the current one works for its users.

## Common mistakes

- Increments that are technical layers: the database first, the interface last.
- An increment so small that all the feedback is about what is missing.
- One big release to everyone after years of work.
- Waiting for perfect. Lego Universe had about 250 people for four to five years; it closed two years
  after its release.

## Example

The Swedish police wanted laptops in their cars, with the systems officers need on the road. An
earlier attempt had taken seven years to reach a first release. This time version 1.0 supported a
couple of crime types and was tested by a handful of police in one region. Other cases were still done
the old way, at the station.
