## Why it matters for a product engineer

Retention is the share of people who come back. It shows whether the product keeps giving value
after the first visit, which no launch or campaign can fake. For engineers, it is also the metric that
decides what to build: work that helps people return compounds, and work that only brings new
visitors has to be repeated.

## How to do it

1. Define "retained" with an action that shows value, at the product's natural rhythm: weekly for a
   planning tool, daily for a language app.
2. Choose the kind of retention: returning on a given day or week, or returning at any time after it.
   Write down which one you use.
3. Split users into groups by how recently they were active: new, current, at risk, dormant. Measure
   how many move between groups each week.
4. Find the rate that moves your main metric most. Often it is not new-user retention.
5. Give one team that rate as its goal, and test changes against it.
6. Report retention by cohort, so the effect of each change is visible.

## Common mistakes

- Counting logins or app opens as "retained".
- Mixing daily, weekly and monthly definitions in one report.
- Working only on new-user retention because it is the first problem you see.
- Retention numbers inflated by reminders that bring people back without value.

## Example

In 2018 Duolingo's daily active users had almost stopped growing. Its growth model split users into seven
states, and showed that the retention of current users, people active in the past week, had five
times the effect on daily users of the next best rate. A new team took that rate as its goal. Mamitsu
does not track usage yet; the first retention question suggested to its maker is which extractions
bring users back. ([Mamitsu in In practice](/in-practice/mamitsu/))
