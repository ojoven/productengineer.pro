## Why it matters for a product engineer

Some errors in thinking are not random. They push in the same direction again and again. We notice
evidence that agrees with us (confirmation bias). We keep funding a project because of what it already
cost (sunk cost). We stay close to the first number we hear (anchoring). We plan as if our project
will go better than similar ones (the planning fallacy). Knowing these names does not protect you. A
process that checks your thinking at the right moment does.

## How to do it

1. Write down your prediction before you see the results of a test or a launch.
2. Before you estimate, look at how long similar projects really took, then adjust from there.
3. Ask what evidence would change your mind, and look for it on purpose.
4. When you decide whether to continue a project, ignore what it has already cost.
5. Ask someone with no stake in the idea to argue against it.

## Common mistakes

- Thinking that knowing about biases makes you free of them.
- Looking only at the average result of past projects, not the worst ones.
- Reading user research only for quotes that support the plan.
- Calling it "commitment" when it is sunk cost.

## Example

In 2003, Levi Strauss started moving to a single SAP system, with a budget of less than $5 million.
In 2008, it took a $192.5 million charge for the project. Researchers at Oxford studied 1,471 IT projects.
The average cost overrun was 27%, but one project in six overran by 200% on average. They recommend
reference class forecasting, a method based on the work of Kahneman and Tversky: estimate from the
results of similar projects, not from your own plan.
