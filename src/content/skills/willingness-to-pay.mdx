## Why it matters for a product engineer

A problem can be real and still not pay for a product. Willingness to pay tells you whether people
value the solution enough to spend money on it, and roughly how much. That decides how much you can
build, for whom, and whether the idea can become a business.

## How to do it

1. Find out what the problem costs people today: time, other tools, mistakes, risk.
2. Ask about what they paid in the past for the same job, not what they would pay in the future.
3. In conversations, say a price and watch the reaction. Ask what would be too cheap to trust and too
   expensive to consider. These are two of the four Van Westendorp questions, and they give a range.
4. Ask for a commitment: a pre-order, a deposit, a paid pilot.
5. When the product is live, test real prices on the pricing page with new visitors.
6. Compare your price with what the [substitutes](/skills-map/discovery/market-research/substitutes/)
   cost, including doing nothing.

## Common mistakes

- "Would you pay $20 a month for this?". Most people say yes and never do.
- Asking only friends, colleagues or other founders.
- Treating a survey range as the final price.
- Setting the price from your costs instead of from the value to the customer.
- Waiting until launch to ask for money at all.

## Example

Soon after Papermark's launch, the first customers asked if they could pay. Iuliia Shnai later said
they paid even though the payment flow was not finished. An unprompted request to pay is the
strongest signal there is, and it turned a weekend project into the business.
([Papermark in In practice](/in-practice/papermark/))
