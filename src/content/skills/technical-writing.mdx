## Why it matters for a product engineer

Code shows what a system does, not why it was built that way or how to use it. Design docs, READMEs,
specs and runbooks carry that knowledge to people you will never talk to, including future you. A
clear document lets a reviewer find a problem while it is still cheap to fix. It also lets a new
teammate start without a meeting. As more code is written by agents, written intent matters more:
it is what both people and agents work from.

## How to do it

1. Name the reader and what they need to do after reading.
2. Start with the context and the goal, then the details.
3. Keep one kind of document per page: a tutorial, a how-to guide, a reference or an explanation.
4. Use short sentences, lists for steps, and a diagram where words get long.
5. Ask someone from the target audience to use the document, and fix where they get stuck.

## Common mistakes

- Writing for yourself, with steps you skip because you already know them.
- A wall of text where a list or a table would be clearer.
- Mixing a tutorial, a reference and a design rationale in one page.
- Documents that are never updated, so readers stop trusting them.

## Example

Malte Ubl describes how Google uses design docs. They are informal, but most follow the same
structure, and the part that matters most is the trade-offs. Non-goals are things that could
reasonably be goals but were chosen not to be. Alternatives considered explain why the other designs
lost. For a larger project, the sweet spot is about 10 to 20 pages, and a one-to-three-page "mini
design doc" is also possible.
