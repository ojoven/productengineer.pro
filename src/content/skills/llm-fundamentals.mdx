## Why it matters for a product engineer

A language model predicts the next token from the tokens before it. That simple fact explains most of
what it does well and most of what goes wrong. It knows what was in its training data, plus what you
put in the context. It writes fluent text even when it has no good answer. Its context window has a
limit, and even inside that limit, attention is not equal. Product engineers who understand this
design better prompts, choose better data to send, and know when a rule or a person is the better
choice.

## How to do it

1. Separate what the model must know from training, and what you must give it in the context.
2. Count tokens for real inputs, and plan for the longest cases, not the average.
3. Put the most important information at the start or the end of the context.
4. Send fewer, more relevant documents instead of everything that might help.
5. Compare the model with a simple baseline, such as a rule or a search, on the same test cases.

## Common mistakes

- Treating the model as a database of facts.
- Assuming that a bigger context window means the model uses all of it well.
- Judging a model from a few good examples.
- Using a model where a fixed rule would be cheaper and always correct.

## Example

In 2023, researchers at Stanford and elsewhere gave models a question and 20 documents, only one of
which contained the answer. They moved that document to different positions. GPT-3.5-Turbo answered
correctly 75.8% of the time when it was first, and 53.8% when it was in the middle. With no documents
at all, it scored 56.1%. These were 2023 models, so test your own. The lesson
is the same: where you put information can matter.
