## Why it matters for a product engineer

Shipping is not the goal; the result is. Accountability means caring whether the work did what it was
meant to do, and owning what happens when it does not. That includes mistakes. An engineer who says
early "I broke this, here is the fix" limits the damage and earns trust. An engineer who hides a
mistake makes the next one more likely, because the team cannot learn from it. Accountability works
best in a team that studies how the system failed, not who to blame.

## How to do it

1. Define what success means before you ship, so you can check it after.
2. Check the result yourself after release: metrics, errors, user feedback.
3. When something goes wrong, say so early, with what you know and what you are doing.
4. Write a blameless postmortem: what happened, why the system allowed it, what will change.
5. Follow the actions to the end, each with an owner and a date.

## Common mistakes

- Treating "shipped" as "done".
- Blaming a person when a process allowed the error.
- Postmortems with actions that nobody owns.
- Apologising without saying what will change.

## Example

On 2 July 2019, a new firewall rule caused errors across Cloudflare's network for 27 minutes. A regular
expression in the rule used all the CPU on the servers that handle web traffic, worldwide. Ten days
later, John Graham-Cumming, who had written the original firewall code years before, published a
detailed postmortem: "We are ashamed of the outage, and sorry for the impact on our customers." It
named the process gap: the standard procedure let a non-emergency rule go to every server at once. It
listed the fixes with dates, including staged rollouts and a check of all 3,868 rules.
