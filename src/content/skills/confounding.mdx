## Why it matters for a product engineer

In product data, the users who do something are rarely like the users who do not. They are more
engaged, more experienced, or on a bigger plan, and those differences also drive the outcome. If you
mistake this for an effect, you build features that do nothing, and then promote them as successes.

## How to do it

1. For any claim like "users who do X do better", ask what makes people do X in the first place.
2. Draw the causes: the possible confounder, the feature and the outcome, with arrows between them.
3. Compare like with like, such as users with similar activity before they used X.
4. Adjust for confounders in the analysis. You can only adjust for what you measured.
5. When the decision is important, run a randomized experiment instead.

## Common mistakes

- Adjusting for a few variables and calling the result causal.
- Adjusting for something that the feature itself changed.
- Ignoring the direction: engagement can cause feature use, not only the other way.
- Trusting a strong association because the sample is large.

## Example

Many observational studies found less heart disease in women who used hormone replacement therapy.
A meta-analysis gave a relative risk of 0.56. Then the large, randomized Women's Health Initiative
trial found a higher risk for women assigned to the therapy. One likely explanation is confounding.
A 2004 study found that women in worse social and economic circumstances, from childhood on, were
less likely to use the therapy, and those circumstances also affect heart disease.
