## Why it matters for a product engineer

Every plan rests on beliefs: users want this, the old system is the bottleneck, this change will not
hurt conversion. Some of these beliefs come from data. Many come from one memorable experience, a
colleague's opinion, or simple repetition. They feel the same from the inside. Writing "evidence" or
"assumption" next to each claim makes the difference visible, and shows which assumptions could sink
the plan if they are wrong. Product engineers can often turn an assumption into evidence quickly, with
a query or a small test.

## How to do it

1. Write the claims behind a decision as short sentences.
2. Mark each one: evidence (with its source) or assumption.
3. For each piece of evidence, ask how strong it is: one anecdote, a survey, or real usage data.
4. Rank the assumptions by risk: how likely they are to be wrong, and how much that would cost.
5. Test the riskiest one first, with the cheapest check that could prove it wrong.

## Common mistakes

- Treating something repeated often as something proven.
- Counting your own experience as evidence for everyone.
- Testing the safe assumptions, because they are easier, and skipping the risky one.
- Keeping an old conclusion after new data contradicts it.

## Example

Microsoft researchers asked 564 developers about common claims in software engineering. The main
source of their beliefs was personal experience. Research papers came second to last. The team then
picked one claim: that distributed teams write code as good as teams in one place. Developers on one
large project mostly disagreed, and on another they mostly agreed. Data from both projects showed only
a very small effect, and not always in the same direction.
