## Why it matters for a product engineer

Every industry has its own way of getting work done: steps, roles, documents and deadlines that
outsiders do not see. A feature that ignores the workflow adds work. A feature that fits one painful
step can be the whole product. Engineers who know the workflow choose better defaults, permissions and
data models.

## How to do it

1. Map the workflow from start to end with the people who do it: the steps, who does each one, which
   documents move, and the deadlines.
2. Watch at least one real run of it, or as much of it as you can.
3. Mark the steps that are slow, risky or repeated by hand.
4. Note the events that start and end the work: a fundraise, a month-end close, a school term. They
   decide when people need your product, and when they leave.
5. Fit the product to the order of the workflow. Ask for what a step needs, when it needs it.
6. Use the industry's own [words](/skills-map/discovery/domain-understanding/domain-language/) for
   each step.

## Common mistakes

- Designing one step and ignoring what happens before and after it.
- Assuming the workflow is the same in every company and country.
- Treating the end of a cycle as churn, when the work itself has ended.
- Learning the workflow from managers instead of the people who do it.

## Example

Papermark follows the shape of a deal. Templates for fundraising, M&A and LP reporting set up the
room. Visitors verify an email or accept an NDA before they read, and the team sees who read which
page. Because deals end, cancelling offers a three-month pause, and the links keep working.
([Papermark in In practice](/in-practice/papermark/))
