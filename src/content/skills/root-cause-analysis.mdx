## Why it matters for a product engineer

A fix at the symptom lets the problem come back in another form. Engineers already trace bugs to their
cause. The same habit, applied to user problems, stops a team from building patches on top of patches.
It also points the fix at something the team can change, such as a process, a default or a missing
owner, and not at a person.

## How to do it

1. State the problem as an observed fact, with its size: "the site was down for 18 hours".
2. Ask why, and answer only with facts you can show. Mark a guess as a guess.
3. Keep asking until you reach something the team can change, usually a process. Five is a habit, not
   a rule.
4. When a problem has two parts, split it and follow each part.
5. Write a fix for each cause, with an owner, and check later that the symptom has stopped.

## Common mistakes

- Stopping at a symptom, or at "human error".
- Looking for one root cause when there are several.
- Finding only the causes the people in the room already know. Bring the people who were there, and
  the data.
- Results that depend on who asks. Different people often find different causes for the same problem.

## Example

After its database outage in January 2017, GitLab published a five-whys analysis. It split the
incident into two problems: why the site was down, and why restoring it took so long. The first chain
did not end at the engineer who deleted the data. It ended at the tool for abuse reports. The second
ended at "there was no ownership": nobody was responsible for testing the backups.
