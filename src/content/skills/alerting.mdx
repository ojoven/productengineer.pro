## Why it matters for a product engineer

An alert should mean that a person needs to act now. When alerts fire for things nobody needs to fix,
people learn to ignore them, and then they miss the one that matters. Good alerting is a product
decision as much as a technical one: it encodes what "broken for users" means, and who must wake up
for it.

## How to do it

1. Alert on symptoms users feel, such as errors and slow responses, not on every possible cause.
2. Tie alerts to your service level objectives, so an alert means the error budget is at risk.
3. Use two speeds: fast burns page someone, slow burns become a ticket.
4. Give every alert an owner and a short note: what it means and the first step.
5. Send everything else, such as single errors and warnings, to a place people read during the day.
6. Review alerts every month. Remove or fix every alert that fired without action.

## Common mistakes

- Paging on CPU or memory when users notice nothing.
- One channel where alerts, logs and messages mix.
- Alerts with no owner.
- Thresholds so sensitive that people silence them.

## Example

Mamitsu's maker does not watch his error tracker. An agent checks Sentry all the time, and for each
new error it opens a Slack thread: is it a real error, and does it need him? Sometimes the agent has a fix
ready before he has read the thread. ([Mamitsu in In practice](/in-practice/mamitsu/))
