## Why it matters for a product engineer

A product goal says what should change, not what to ship. A goal like "more new users finish setup"
leaves engineers room to find the best solution, and it shows when a finished feature did not work.
A goal like "ship a setup wizard" is met on the day it ships, whether or not anything changed.

## How to do it

1. Start from the outcome: what users will do differently, or what the business will gain.
2. Write the objective in words, then add two to four measurable results. At the end, each result is
   either met or not.
3. Set a period, usually a quarter, and a date to check.
4. Add a guardrail: a number that must not get worse while you work on the goal.
5. Let the team choose the solution. A goal that names a feature is a plan, not a goal.
6. At the end, grade the results honestly and write down what you learned.

## Common mistakes

- Goals that are lists of features.
- So many goals that none of them guides a decision.
- Results nobody can measure, or that the team cannot influence.
- Changing the goal at the end of the period so that it looks met.

## Example

When Mamitsu's maker packaged it, the goal for the two weeks was not a list of features. It was a
product the maker would feel confident selling: one that works, holds load and is easy to use from an
app or an agent. Holding load became a daily load test, because it was the condition for selling.
([Mamitsu in In practice](/in-practice/mamitsu/))
