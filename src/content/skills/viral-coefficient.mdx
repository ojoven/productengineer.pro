## Why it matters for a product engineer

The viral coefficient is the number of new users each user brings: invitations sent per user, times
the share of invitations that turn into new users. Both parts are product decisions: when the product
suggests sharing, what a share contains, and what the new person sees. A coefficient above 1 is rare.
But even at 0.3, each user you acquire is worth about 1.4 users in total. Cycle time matters too: a
faster loop brings the next users sooner.

## How to do it

1. Measure the two parts separately: invitations or shares per user, and the conversion of each.
2. Measure the cycle time: days from a user's signup to the signups of the people they bring.
3. Track by cohort and by channel, because the average hides strong and weak loops.
4. Improve the part with more room to grow, and test each change against a holdout group.
5. Count only invitees who become active, not every signup.

## Common mistakes

- Reporting one number, without its two parts and its cycle time.
- Pushing more invitations at the cost of their quality, until people ignore them.
- Expecting growth that feeds itself from a coefficient below 1.
- Measuring the loop on early adopters, then expecting the same from everyone.

## Example

Sinan Aral and Dylan Walker ran a randomized trial with a Facebook app, with 9,687 users and their 1.4
million friends. Some users got no viral features, some got automatic notifications to friends, and
some also got personal invitations. Personal invitations brought more adoption per message. But
automatic notifications were sent far more often, so they brought more adoption in total.
Notifications raised peer adoption by 246%, and personal invitations added only another 98%.
