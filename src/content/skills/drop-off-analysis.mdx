## Why it matters for a product engineer

New users leave at specific places, and the funnel shows where. Research then explains why. A
product engineer can join the funnel with errors, load times and replays, so the team does not
redesign a page that was actually broken. And not every drop-off is bad: sometimes the right users
are leaving at the right moment.

## How to do it

1. Define the first experience as a funnel of events, for one cohort of new users, with one
   denominator.
2. Find the step with the largest absolute loss, then split it by device, browser, country and
   entry point.
3. Watch replays of sessions that stopped there, and of sessions that continued.
4. Ask what the people who left needed at that moment. Some needed a fix, some needed an earlier
   answer.
5. Change one thing, then look at the whole funnel, not only the step you changed.
6. Repeat the analysis after every important change.

## Common mistakes

- Comparing funnels built from different cohorts or definitions.
- Assuming every drop-off is a problem to remove.
- Fixing one step, and not checking whether the loss moved to another.
- Explaining a drop from the numbers alone, without watching or asking users.

## Example

In GOV.UK's Carer's Allowance service, about 5% of first-time users left at a legal disclaimer two
screens before the end. It told them that the person they cared for could lose some benefits. The
team moved a shorter, clearer version to the start and added a way out. Drop-off on that page rose
to 9%, but abandonment fell elsewhere, and there was no fall in applications. In the week of the
change, the completion rate rose 6%, and it later passed 80%.
