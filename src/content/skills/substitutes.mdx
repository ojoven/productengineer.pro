## Why it matters for a product engineer

Users compare you with what they do today, and that is often not a product: an email, a spreadsheet,
a person, or nothing. The substitute sets the bar. It tells you how much faster, cheaper or easier the
product must be before anyone switches.

## How to do it

1. For one job, ask people what they did the last time it came up. Write down every answer, including
   "nothing".
2. Add the less obvious ones: manual work, an internal script, a general tool like a spreadsheet, a
   service, a colleague who does it.
3. For each one, note what it costs and what it does well. A free substitute that everyone already
   knows is hard to beat.
4. Find the moment the substitute fails: a deadline, more volume, a second person. That moment is where
   your product can win.
5. Write in one sentence why someone would leave the most common substitute for your product.

## Common mistakes

- Listing only products in your own category.
- Saying "we have no competition". There is always a substitute, even living with the problem.
- Forgetting the cost of switching: learning a new tool, moving data, telling the team.
- Comparing features when the substitute wins on effort.

## Example

For Zernio's buyers, the substitutes are building each integration in-house, or connectors like Zapier
and n8n that still need your own developer app ([Zernio](/in-practice/zernio/)). For someone anxious
right now, the alternative to RelievApp is not another app but a breathing video, a walk or the
phone's feed ([RelievApp](/in-practice/relievapp-part-1/)). In a case the Christensen Institute
describes, a fast-food chain found that its morning milkshakes competed with bananas and bagels, not
with other milkshakes.
