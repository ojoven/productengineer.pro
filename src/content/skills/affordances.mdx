## Why it matters for a product engineer

An interface has to show what can be clicked, dragged or changed. A button that looks like a button,
a link that looks like a link and a handle that looks draggable all save people from guessing. Many of
these cues are CSS decisions that engineers make: underline or not, border or not, a cursor, a focus
style.

## How to do it

1. Give every interactive element a strong cue: a button shape, link colour and underline, a visible
   handle.
2. Keep the cues consistent. If blue text is a link in one place, it is a link everywhere.
3. Do not make decorative elements look interactive, or interactive ones look decorative.
4. Show hover, focus and pressed states, so the interface answers when someone acts.
5. Test findability: give people a task and watch where they look before they click.

## Common mistakes

- Flat text that is secretly a link.
- Ghost buttons, with a thin outline and no fill, for the main action.
- Icons with no label for actions people rarely use.
- Removing focus outlines, so keyboard users cannot see where they are.

## Example

In NN/g's study, people were asked to cancel a car rental on two versions of Hertz's home page. On the
version with strong cues, their eyes went to the right tab. On the version with weak cues, their eyes
also wandered to footer links, promotions and the form next to the tab: they had seen the target but
did not recognise it.
