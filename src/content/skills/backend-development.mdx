## Why it matters for a product engineer

The backend holds the rules of the business, the data, sign-in and the connections to other systems.
It must be correct, secure and easy to observe. A product engineer moves between frontend and backend
to deliver a feature end to end, so they do not wait for another team to add a field or an endpoint.

## How to do it

1. Put the business rules in one place on the server. Never trust a check that only runs in the
   browser.
2. Keep configuration, such as keys and URLs, in the environment, never in the code.
3. Treat the database, email, payments and queues as services you connect to, so you can replace one
   without rewriting the rest.
4. Move slow work out of the request: a job queue can resize images or send emails while the user
   continues.
5. Validate every input at the edge and return clear errors with the right status codes.
6. Log what you need to answer "what happened to this user's request?", without personal data.

## Common mistakes

- Business rules copied into the frontend and the backend, until they disagree.
- Secrets in the repository.
- Slow work inside the request, so the page waits for an email to send.
- Building your own sign-in, payments or email delivery when a proven service exists.

## Example

Papermark, an open-source data room product, runs as a Next.js app on Vercel. On Starter Story its
founders named the rest: Postgres on PlanetScale, Trigger.dev for background jobs, Resend for email and
Stripe for payments. The code adds Tinybird for page-view events. The app holds the product logic, and
each of the other parts is a service it calls. ([Papermark in In practice](/in-practice/papermark/))
