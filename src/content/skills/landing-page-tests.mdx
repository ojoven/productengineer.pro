## Why it matters for a product engineer

A landing page is the cheapest product an engineer can ship. In an afternoon it tests two things
together: whether the message is clear, and whether anyone wants what it promises. It does not prove
that people will use the product, but it stops you from building for months with no signal at all.

## How to do it

1. Write one hypothesis: who it is for, the problem, and the promise.
2. Build one page: a headline in the user's words, three benefits, and one action that costs the
   visitor something, such as an email address, a chosen plan or a pre-order.
3. Decide before you start what result means "continue", for example a share of visitors who leave
   an email.
4. Bring visitors from where your target users already are. Friends and colleagues do not count.
5. Measure actions, not visits. Then write to the people who signed up and ask them what they hoped
   for.
6. Change one thing at a time, such as the headline or the price, and compare.

## Common mistakes

- Counting visits or time on the page instead of sign-ups.
- Traffic from the wrong audience, such as other founders on a launch site.
- Calling three sign-ups a validation.
- Promising something you will not build. Tell people clearly that the product is not ready yet.
- Testing several messages at once with too few visitors to tell them apart.

## Example

Before Buffer existed, Joel Gascoigne published two pages: one that described the product, and one
that asked for an email address. He tweeted the link. People left their email, so he added a page
with plans and prices between the two. Visitors still clicked through, and a few chose paid plans. He
then built the first version in seven weeks and had his first paying customer four days after the
launch.
