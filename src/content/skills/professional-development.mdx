## Why it matters for a product engineer

A product engineer's work touches many fields: code, users, data, design, business. Nobody is strong in
all of them, and the useful mix changes every few years. Without a plan, people learn whatever the
current job needs and stop there. With a plan, you choose the next gap on purpose and practise it on
real work. Dan Luu argues that steady, deliberate practice is rare enough that modest effort already
puts you ahead in most skills.

## How to do it

1. Use a map of skills, such as this one, to mark what you are strong in and what is missing.
2. Pick one or two gaps for the next three months, and connect each to real work.
3. Practise on a real product, at work or on a side project, where results show up.
4. Learn the layer under your tools: how the database, the browser or the model behaves.
5. Write about what you learn, and ask people who are better than you for feedback.

## Common mistakes

- Collecting courses and certificates without using them on real work.
- Learning only technical skills because they feel safer.
- Picking skills by what is popular instead of what your work needs.
- Never checking, months later, whether a skill actually improved.

## Example

Papermark's Iuliia Shnai moved from academia to building products without knowing how to code. She
learned by building on open-source projects, and she made her own work open source too. Zernio's
Miquel Palet came from a technical background, and credits Ucademy's non-technical founders with most
of what he learned. Both learned from the side they did not know yet.
([Papermark](/in-practice/papermark/), [Zernio](/in-practice/zernio/))
