## Why it matters for a product engineer

Plans should change when the evidence changes. But a team that changes direction at every signal
learns nothing, because no direction lasts long enough to test. Adapting well means a stable vision,
a plan that changes to reach it, and a fixed rhythm for changing it. Engineers feel both failures:
rewrites after every change of direction, or months on a plan that everyone knows is wrong.

## How to do it

1. Separate the layers. The vision says where you want to be in years. The product strategy connects
   it to the business. The roadmap is the current plan.
2. Before you start, write what evidence would change the plan.
3. Review the plan on a fixed rhythm, such as every month or quarter, not after every comment.
4. When the evidence is strong, change the plan, and say what you learned and what stays the same.
5. Give each direction enough time and enough users to produce evidence.

## Common mistakes

- No strategy at all, so the team only chases features. Marty Cagan describes a team that felt it had
  done exactly that for a year.
- Changing direction on opinions, including your own.
- Never changing, because the plan was announced.
- Changing the vision when only the plan was wrong.

## Example

RelievApp changed its promise five times in five weeks. When a new version takes an hour, changing
direction is affordable. Its maker's own conclusion is that cheap change is not the same as learning.
The next step is measurement: every session sends the same events, so use can decide what stays.
